# References

## Primary Data Sources

- **ANES 2016 Time Series Study**
  https://electionstudies.org/data-center/2016-time-series-study/
  The core dataset. Variables for ideology, party ID, institutional trust,
  presidential vote choice.

- **ANES Cumulative Data File (1948–2020)**
  https://electionstudies.org/data-center/anes-cumulative-data-file/
  Useful for trend lines on institutional trust over time.

- **Democracy Fund VOTER Survey (2016)**
  https://www.voterstudygroup.org/data
  Panel data. Identifies voters who supported Bernie in the primary and Trump
  in the general. Critical for the "Bernie → Trump" claim.

- **Pew Research Political Typology**
  https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/
  Pre-built clusters (2014, 2017, 2021, 2023). "Stressed Sideliners" and
  "Populist Right" are the key low-trust independent clusters.

- **CCES (Cooperative Congressional Election Study)**
  https://cces.gov.harvard.edu/
  Large N, good for sub-group analysis. Includes trust and ideology items.

## Books and Long-form

- Drutman, Lee. *Breaking the Two-Party Doom Loop.* Oxford, 2020.
  Argues both parties have abandoned large portions of the electorate; most
  relevant for the "orphaned low-trust voter" argument.

- Grossmann, Matt, and David Hopkins. *Asymmetric Politics.* Oxford, 2016.
  Democrats as coalition of groups, Republicans as ideological movement.
  Relevant: why Democratic "move to center" advice is structurally asymmetric.

- Hochschild, Arlie. *Strangers in Their Own Land.* New Press, 2016.
  Ethnographic. The "deep story" of Tea Party / Trump voters is a trust story,
  not a policy moderation story.

- Judis, John, and Ruy Teixeira. *The Emerging Democratic Majority.* 2002.
  The theoretical foundation for the "win the center" strategy — useful as
  the thesis to argue against.

- Teixeira, Ruy. *The Optimistic Leftist.* 2017.
  Follow-up. Already beginning to acknowledge the limits of the 1D model.

## Academic Articles

- Achen, Christopher, and Larry Bartels. "It Feels Like We're Thinking."
  *The Forum,* 2006. Voters rationalize, don't deliberate. Trust > policy.

- Ahler, Douglas, and Gaurav Sood. "The Parties in Our Heads." *JOP,* 2018.
  Voters wildly overestimate party extremism. Relevant to why "centrist policy"
  doesn't land as moderate.

- Hetherington, Marc. *Why Trust Matters.* Princeton, 2005.
  Direct treatment of institutional trust as a political variable. Essential.

- Hetherington, Marc, and Thomas Rudolph. *Why Washington Won't Work.* Chicago, 2015.
  Trust polarization → governing dysfunction. Shows trust is not a noise variable.

- Mutz, Diana. "Status Threat, Not Economic Hardship, Explains the 2016
  Presidential Vote." *PNAS,* 2018.
  Economic anxiety vs. status threat vs. institutional distrust as 2016
  predictors. Supports the trust-over-policy argument.

## Journalism and Commentary

- Hayes, Chris. "The Swamp Has Won." Various, 2017+.
  Anti-corruption as populist left-right bridge issue.

- Klein, Ezra. *Why We're Polarized.* Simon & Schuster, 2020.
  Identity > policy in partisan sorting. Relevant but mostly about *partisan*
  voters, not independents.

- Shor, David, various interviews 2020–2022.
  The "popularism" argument — often invoked for centrist pivot. Worth engaging
  critically: his data is real but the prescription assumes the 1D model.

## Notes

> Key tension to address: David Shor's popularism argument uses real polling
> data showing unpopular positions hurt Democrats. That's true on the margins
> among *high-trust* persuadables. It does not address *low-trust swing voters*,
> who are not responsive to policy position convergence. The thesis is
> not "policy doesn't matter" — it's "policy position convergence toward the
> center does not address the trust-deficit cohort, which is where the actual
> swing is."
