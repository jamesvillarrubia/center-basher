Who Made the 2026 Majorities

2026 midterms · the data

Moderates didn't make the 2026 majorities. The wave did.

Third Way says moderate Democrats delivered the House and Senate. On the same ballot, Democrats backed by moderate groups ran no further ahead than Democrats backed by progressive groups, and the winners Third Way claims ran on platforms to its left.

The short version

  • Moderate-backed and progressive-backed Democrats tied. Across 195 House races, nominees backed by the New Democrats, Blue Dogs or WelcomePAC ran 0.1 points further ahead of Harris than nominees backed by the Progressive Caucus, Working Families or Justice Democrats in the same state. The moderate-backed nominees raised almost twice as much money, and the tie holds after accounting for both sides' money, small donors, PACs and super PACs.
  • Every kind of Democrat rode the same wave. 412 of the 417 House Democrats who faced a Republican ran ahead of Harris's 2024 margin, by 14.4 points on average.
  • Third Way's own number is the national average. It says its House picks ran 15 points ahead of Harris. So did the average House Democrat. In the Republican seats Democrats flipped, the nominees the moderate groups did not back ran further ahead, 18.6 points against 14.6.
  • Third Way's "moderates" don't hold Third Way's positions. All 19 winners it claims took positions to its left. Talarico, its star example, disagrees with Third Way on 9 of 13 issues.
  • Red-state Democrats ran far ahead whether Third Way claimed them or not. Ten red-state Senate Democrats it never mentioned, most of them long shots, ran 18.7 points ahead of Harris. Its seven claimed moderates ran 20.8 ahead.
How to read the charts. Almost every chart measures one thing: how much better a Democrat did in 2026 than Harris did in the same state or district in 2024. A Democrat who won by 10 points where Harris lost by 5 "ran 15 points ahead of Harris." We judge candidates by how far they beat that mark, not by whether they won, because winning mostly depends on how red the seat is.

How we picked the races, and what that could bias

  • Third Way's labels were assigned after the votes were counted. Every one of its 19 claimed moderates won, and the New Democrat and WelcomePAC picks who lost are missing from its count. Any label handed out to winners will look like it predicts winning, which is why the charts above include the unclaimed and the losers.
  • Third Way's moderates ran on redder ground. The median Harris margin in their races was −3.1, against +6.4 for the nominees it calls "leftier." Across the Senate map, redder states swung further toward Democrats. Every comparison of labels above adjusts for each seat's partisan lean.
  • The 44 contested races are not a typical slice of the House. We picked the 30 House races whose primaries had both a progressive-backed and a moderate-backed candidate. Compared with the other 387, these races are bluer (median Harris margin +6.8 against −10.5), more often open seats (57% against 14%) and more often won (93% against 54%). Charts about the whole House use all 417 races for that reason.
  • The 14 Senate races were picked before the election. We used every race the Cook Political Report did not rate as safe on Nov 2.
  • Maine does not explain the Senate gap. Jackson replaced Platner in late July and had about three months as nominee. Leaving Maine out, Third Way's moderates still ran 12.7 points further ahead of Harris than its "leftier" nominees.
  • Some votes were still being counted. On Nov 5, 101 of the 417 House races were under 90% counted, and those races run 2.2 points lower so far. The main House results hold in the 316 races at least 90% counted.
  • Only nominees are in the data. Candidates who lost their primaries are not, so these comparisons cannot tell us how a different nominee would have done.

What the data can't show

  • Progressive positions did not raise margins either. The data support "progressive nominees won and paid no measurable penalty." They do not support "progressive positions caused the gains."
  • Establishment backing helped in the House. House nominees the party establishment backed before the primary beat a history-based baseline by 6.0 points (95% range +1.2 to +10.8). Backing is not the same as moderation: every one of them still scored to Third Way's left.
  • Money goes with bigger margins, but it also chases races that already look good. Outraising the Republican tenfold goes with about 2.3 more points, and super PAC help goes with about 0.14 points per dollar per voter. Together they explain about 8% of the differences between House races, against 0.25% for moderate or progressive backing. Donors and super PACs pour money into races they expect to win, so not all of that 8% is money causing votes.
  • Win-loss records track the map, not the platform. We judge nominees by how far they beat Harris, because whether a Democrat wins depends mostly on how red the seat is. The most progressive nominees who lost, Nixon in Florida, Dunlap in Maine's 2nd and Pearson in Tennessee's 9th, ran where Harris lost, and they still beat her margin by 7 to 20 points.

How we know

Races. All 417 Democrat-vs-Republican House races, plus 14 competitive Senate races and 30 House races with contested Democratic primaries for the platform scoring. Vote margins from CNN race pages on Nov 5 2026; wins and losses from CNN's calls on Nov 6. Alaska and Mississippi were still uncalled on Nov 6, with Peltola and Colom ahead.

Platform score. 13 issues: Medicare for All, a billionaire tax, no corporate PAC money, no AIPAC money or conditions on Israel aid, the Green New Deal, a $17 minimum wage, ending the filibuster, expanding the Supreme Court, student debt relief or free college, breaking up monopolies, no crypto money, universal childcare or pre-K, and opposing Schumer or Jeffries as leader. Two coders scored each nominee separately and without seeing results (Cohen's κ 0.45, no disagreements on direction). A second pass over rendered campaign-site captures for the 23 least-documented nominees added 10 positions; its two coders agreed on 99.3% of cells (κ 0.94). Gaps are filled by empirical-Bayes shrinkage toward a prediction from pre-primary facts.

Yardsticks. Harris 2024; Biden 2020; the prior race for the seat; the 2022 midterm; an expected margin built from Biden 2020 and the prior race only; and the 2026 statewide House vote and governor race on the same ballot.
