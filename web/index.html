The Center Doesn't Exist

Why "swing voters" aren't moderates — they're the disillusioned

The conventional story

Voters on a line

Every campaign consultant believes the same thing: there's a big block of moderate voters in the middle, and if you soften your positions to win them over, you win. "Don't scare the moderates." "Move to the center." It's repeated so often that nobody checks whether it's true. So we checked.

The longer version

Political analysts love a simple left-right spectrum. Independents sit in the middle. To win them, you move to the center. The whole strategy rests on one assumption: that the voters in play differ from you on left-right, so a horizontal move closes the gap. Everything below tests that assumption — and finds the gap runs the other way.

Figure 1 · 1D spectrum

On this view, "Independent" = "moderate." Winning them means triangulating toward the middle on policy.

A different lens

Add trust. The map changes.

What if we plot the same voters on two axes: left-right ideology and a 3-item index of trust in government — does it do what's right, run for everyone, spend money wisely? Data from the 2016 American National Election Study (n=3,301).

Figure 2 · Ideology × trust

The "center" empties out. Most voters who look moderate on policy actually sit in the low-trust quadrant — not the moderate one. The distance to them is vertical, not horizontal — and that's the gap a left-right move can't close.

Trust, not ideology, is what separated them.

Primary → general

The general election moved each coalition — in different directions.

A candidate's primary voters aren't their general voters. Clinton's primary voters were her most trusting (mean 0.32); the broader fall electorate pulled her coalition's trust down to 0.28 while her ideology barely moved. Trump went the other way — from the primary to the general his voters shifted toward the center on ideology (+0.51 → +0.46), while their low trust held roughly flat (0.12 → 0.14).

Hollow dot = primary-voter average, filled dot = general-election average, the line is the shift — drawn over the same party blobs as Figure 2. Sanders isn't on the general ballot, so only his primary centroid is shown. Markers are means; on the left, higher trust "echoes" up toward 0.6, which is why Clinton's primary base starts high before the general dilutes it.

The contestable ground

Competing for voters means competing for the moveable ones.

Locked-in voters don't decide elections — moveable ones do. Two kinds are actually in play: people likely to vote but still undecided (persuasion targets) and committed partisans who might not turn out (turnout targets). Here the blobs are only those moveable voters; the dots are the candidate primary centroids from Figure 3.

Both moveable clusters have nearly the same shape: centered on moderates but tilting right (42% lean right vs 24% left) and low-trust (~0.19). The densest part sits right-of-center and low-trust — right next to Trump's coalition: 34% of moveable voters are right-and-low-trust versus just 16% left-and-low-trust. That mass sits far below Clinton's coalition (0.32), inside the low-trust band shared by Sanders (0.22) and Trump (0.12) — which is why a low-trust appeal, not a move to the policy center, is what reaches it.

Figure 5 · The contestable ground

Blobs = moveable voters only (locked-in voters removed). Filled dot = candidate's primary-voter median, hollow = mean. The split panels below show the two types are nearly identical in position — both tilt right-of-center and low-trust. The contest isn't about ideology; it's about who reaches that low-trust, slightly-right terrain.

How campaigns chase these voters

The playbook already has two plays — on the wrong axes

We've just met the contestable voters (Figure 5): the persuadables (likely to vote, undecided) and the wavering partisans (decided, but low-turnout). Campaigns don't ignore them — there's a standard playbook. Every modern field operation sorts the electorate on partisanship × how often you vote and runs two plays: persuasion on the swing column (move their choice) and GOTV on the base's low-frequency rows (move their turnout). Those two plays line up exactly with Figure 5's two groups — and they're the two levers we're about to formalize. But look at the axes: partisanship and frequency. Trust isn't on this map.

Figure 5B · The campaign playbook

And look at the cell that gets crossed out: low-frequency persuadable voters — "not worth contacting," because they rarely vote. That's the low-trust corner of Figure 5's contestable mass — the disaffected the playbook writes off as a lost cause. We'll argue they rarely vote because no one has earned their trust, and that the missing axis — trust — is the one that decides which of the two plays can even work.

Who vs. whether

Ideology decides who you pick. How much you care decides whether you show up. Policy is neither.

Winning an election is really two separate problems, and people mix them up constantly: first, of the people who show up, getting them to pick you; second, getting people to show up at all. These don't respond to the same things — and that turns out to be the whole ballgame.

The data behind it

Here's the engine behind everything above. On the left-right axis, predicting who someone voted for (Trump vs Clinton) is easy: ideology dominates — 3.7× stronger than trust. So the map decides choice. But predicting whether they voted at all (turnout matched to the L2 voter file, not self-report) is a different question with a different answer: ideology's power collapses to almost exactly zero — and so does trust's. Neither axis on the map predicts turnout.

What does? Not policy — and not "engagement" as a lever either. Interest tracks turnout (79% of the very interested voted vs 54% of the barely interested), but that's a symptom, not a cause: once you control for how much someone cares who wins, their feelings toward the candidates, plus age and education, campaign interest's own effect collapses to near zero. The real driver is caring — those who "care a good deal" turned out 81% vs 48% for those who didn't. And caring is something candidates manufacture by making voters feel strongly (and coverage amplifies) — not something you create by sliding to the policy center. If anything, centrism dampens it.

Figure 6 · Who vs. whether

Left: who you'd vote for, colored by P(Trump) — shifts sharply left-to-right, so ideology decides choice. Right: vote-validated turnout (71% real vs an inflated 85% self-report). It tracks engagement, while ideology (0.03) and trust (0.02) move it essentially zero — but read the engagement bars as a marker, not a lever: with "care who wins," candidate affect, age and education in the model, interest's own effect falls to ~0.04. Turnout runs on caring and candidate-driven affect, which sit upstream — the next sections trace what actually shifts it.

Can a better policy platform get more people to vote? Campaigns have run real experiments for decades — knock on this door, mail this flyer, then check who actually voted. The verdict is blunt: what gets people to the polls is being contacted and feeling something. A mailer explaining your health-care plan does basically nothing. You don't talk people into voting with policy.

The data behind it

Decades of randomized field experiments (Gerber & Green's Get Out the Vote meta-analyses) measured exactly what moves turnout — and the answer is contact, not content. A door-knock adds ~4 points; a social-pressure "your neighbors will know" mailer adds ~8. The policy message — the thing a centrist pivot changes — adds essentially zero. Turnout is a mobilization problem, not a positioning one.

Figure 6B · What actually shifts turnout

Causal estimates, not correlations: pooled randomized GOTV experiments. Every lever that works is contact or social pressure; the message's policy content moves turnout ≈0. So "move to the center" can't buy turnout — and at the candidate level the real swings (Obama 2008; the 66.6% of 2020, highest since 1904) come from candidates voters feel something about. The positional lever that actually moves the contestable is their choice, via trust (Figure 7) — which is the whole case for matching the base's trust instead of chasing the center.

Figure 6C · The turnout hump (stakes × efficacy)

Zoom in on the gettable — low-engagement voters who don't reliably show up. Two opposite gradients cross: stakes rises with grievance (the aggrieved care more, not less); efficacy falls (the aggrieved have given up that voting matters); and turnout peaks in the middle where you have enough of both. The two failure modes are opposite — the trusting check out from complacency ("doesn't matter who wins"), the aggrieved check out from alienation ("my vote won't matter"). Mobilization isn't one move: stakes for the complacent; efficacy for the aggrieved. Telling someone who's given up that the stakes are high does nothing — they already know.

The contestable bloc, disentangled

Two contestable groups, two levers — and trust gates both.

Figure 6 gave us two levers: engagement decides whether you vote; ideology + trust decide who you pick. The mistake is to point those levers at "low-trust voters" as one bloc. They're not. In 2016 low trust was 63% of the electorate, and most of it was the Republican base distrusting a Democratic government — not contestable at all. The genuinely up-for-grabs voters are the anti-establishment Democrats, independents, and Sanders supporters who sit low on trust.

Inside that group, split it the way Figure 5 did. The engaged contestable (independents and Sanders voters who turn out) are a choice problem — and the chart below, with the Republican base netted out, shows the choice is decided on trust: Clinton kept 90% of high-trust non-Republicans but only 68% of low-trust ones, bleeding 22 points to Trump and third parties. The disengaged contestable (the Pew "Sideliners," next section) are instead a turnout problem — but they're anti-establishment too, so mobilizing them only pays off if you aren't the establishment candidate. Same gate, both levers: trust.

That's why "move to the center" fails here. Policy-centering slides you left-right; it does nothing to your position on trust. Clinton went left on policy to absorb Sanders and up toward the establishment — the $225k Goldman speeches, the private email server, the Foundation — moving the one axis that gated both groups in exactly the wrong direction.

Figure 7 · The contestable bloc, disentangled

With Republicans removed, the trust effect is no longer "low-trust = GOP": among non-Republicans who voted, Clinton's retention slides from 90% (high trust) to 68% (low trust) — 22 points lost to Trump and third parties, with abstention on top. Pure independents flip outright (27/49 Trump at low trust → 55/23 Clinton at high), and Sanders primary voters came home only 77%.

Correlation, handled carefully. A snapshot can't prove that a different trust posture would have moved these voters — distrust may drive the vote, or partisanship may color professed distrust, and 2016 trust readings are partly an artifact of who held the White House. Netting out Republicans removes the biggest confound; the causal claim leans on the defection that actually happened (Figure 11) and on evidence that voters adopt their preferred candidate's signals over time (Lenz 2012), not on this chart alone.

How much can you change once the candidate is decided?

Once two known candidates are set, the campaign can't move much

Okay — can you change people's minds in the fall by repositioning? This has been tested directly: 49 field experiments. Between two well-known candidates, all the ads and door-knocks combined move vote choice by roughly zero. By the time you've got two famous nominees, the election runs on who they already are — not what they say in the last stretch. The decision that actually mattered was made earlier, at the nomination.

The data behind it

Step back and ask the scaling question: how much of the result is fixed by who's on the ballot versus what the campaign does after? The answer from the field-experiment literature is stark. Kalla & Broockman (2018) pooled 49 field experiments and found the best estimate of the persuasive effect of campaign contact and advertising on candidate choice in general elections is zero. Add that policy positioning moves turnout ≈0 too (Figure 6B), and both levers a campaign actually controls round to nothing — between two well-known candidates.

Set that next to the candidate's priors. Within Clinton's own reachable coalition, trust position alone tracked a ~22-point swing in the vote (Figure 7). So the thing that moves the election is who you nominate — their fixed perception, their trust position — not the persuasion and policy moves that come after. The malleable window was the primary (where persuasion can bite, because the candidate is still being defined); by the general, the die was cast.

Figure 8B · The nominee vs. the campaign

A deliberate apples-to-scale comparison: the left bar is a positional association (how much trust position tracks the reachable coalition's vote, Fig 7); the right is a causal campaign effect (Kalla & Broockman, 49 experiments). They measure different things — shown together only to make the magnitude gap legible. The point survives the caveat: the lever is the nomination. This is why "drag a high-trust candidate to the well" was never really on the table — which is exactly what the next section shows.

The other group — the disengaged contestable

Swing voters aren't centrists. They're disaffected.

Figure 7 was the engaged contestable — the ones who vote, where trust decides their choice. This is the other group: the disengaged contestable, where trust gates the turnout lever instead. Before we ask "what if Bernie was on the ballot," we have to make sure this bloc is who we say it is. Our ANES moveable voters sit low on trust (≈0.19) — but that's one survey. Two others, built completely differently, say the same.

Pew's "Stressed Sideliners" — the only one of nine typology groups with no partisan lean, and the least engaged — are split 45% / 45% between the parties (not centrist, just torn), lean left on economics (74% want a $15 wage), and are defined by distrust: 83% say the economy "unfairly favors the powerful." The Knight nonvoter study lands in the same place (~even, disaffected). Three datasets, one answer: the contestable bloc is disengaged and distrustful, not moderate.

Figure 8 · Swing ≠ center

This is the turnout lever's population — and it's the hardest to reach, because turnout isn't a position you can move toward. The Sideliners are the lowest-turnout typology not for where they sit on the left-right map but because nothing has made them care — and caring is what drives turnout (Figure 6). You can't manufacture it by centering; it comes from a candidate who makes people feel something. At 83% "the system favors the powerful," the only thing that would is an anti-establishment signal — a trust move, not a policy one. Both contestable groups bottleneck on the same axis. The next sections trace what actually shifts turnout — and the consolidation that decided 2016.

Political gravity

The contestable mass sits low. Candidates orbit high.

Here's the geometry made literal. The terrain is the moveable voter mass — undecided and disengaged voters, the only people actually up for grabs (n=1,028) — and it sits low on trust, near the floor of the map. Gravity here is trust-proximity: a coalition pulls the contestable in to the degree it sits near them on the vertical axis, regardless of left-right.

Sanders' primary coalition was 40% moveable — three times Clinton's 13%. His blob sat directly on top of the contestable mass; Clinton's floated above it in the high-trust zone, too far up to exert much pull no matter her policy positions. When Sanders wasn't on the general ballot, that overlap had nowhere obvious to go.

Figure 9 · Coalition overlap

Among Sanders' 339 primary voters: 62% voted Clinton, 21% didn't vote, 7% voted Trump. The Bernie→Trump voters had near-centrist ideology (x ≈ 0) — they didn't move right on policy. They moved on trust.

Figure 10 · Sanders redistribution

Research basis: Lenz (2012) Follow the Leader documents that voters adopt the positions of their favored candidate over time as identity function — not the other way around. Proximity in 2D predicts who they follow.

The arithmetic

The pivot was consolidation, not the center.

So 2016 wasn't a story of Clinton failing to win over moderate Republicans. It's a story of her failing to hold the people who were already hers. Enough Bernie Sanders primary voters flipped to Trump — or stayed home — to swing Wisconsin, Michigan, and Pennsylvania, and with them the election. In every one of those three states, the Sanders-to-Trump switchers alone outnumbered Trump's margin of victory. And those switchers weren't conservatives talked rightward — they were anti-establishment voters who were never going to rally behind the ultimate establishment candidate.

The data behind it

Put it all in one unit — votes — in the three states that decided the Electoral College. Trump won them by a combined 77,744. We re-ran the vote-validated CES ourselves (it reproduces the 12% national Sanders→Trump rate exactly) and applied each state's rate to its certified Sanders primary vote. The Sanders→Trump defectors alone: ~61k in Wisconsin (2.7× the margin), ~56k in Michigan (5.3×), ~131k in Pennsylvania (3.0×) — they exceeded Trump's margin in every decisive state. And that's before counting the ~30% of Sanders primary voters who didn't vote at all (validated).

So the decisive problem wasn't winning new ground in the ideological center — it was holding a coalition that was already there. And per Figure 6, a center-policy pivot only competes for the choice of the already-engaged; it can't mobilize a disaffected, low-trust bloc like the un-consolidated Sanders voters — the very people who defected or stayed home.

Figure 11 · The arithmetic

Bounded claim, not a "Bernie would have won" proof. Sources: Schaffner/CCES via NPR, NBC News. Primary crossover isn't unique (≈12% of GOP primary voters went Clinton; ~25% of Clinton's 2008 voters later backed McCain), and a Sanders nomination was never stress-tested against a general-election GOP assault. The defensible point is the arithmetic of consolidation: the bloc that didn't come home was several times the size of the margin that decided it.

The strategic choice

You can't drag a high-trust candidate into the well. Start there instead.

Put the levers together and a candidate has two ways to win the contestable: change who they pick (choice) or change whether they vote (turnout). For a high-trust, establishment candidate both are nearly closed. Choice is gated by trust-proximity, and perceived trust is sticky — Clinton's genuinely progressive record never pulled her down off the establishment perch (Fig 12), so the well's vote stays out of reach. And turnout can't rescue it: a policy pivot moves turnout ≈0 (Fig 6B), while actually mobilizing the low-trust well just turns out voters who break against her (Fig 7). She is capped — and most of that cap is set before the campaign even starts.

A candidate who starts in the well inverts both. They win its choice for free (trust-proximity, no repositioning needed), and — being an outsider — they self-generate the affect and earned media that are the only proven movers of turnout. What they give up is institutional support, money, and name recognition — but those move neither lever. It's a good trade: you swap the things that don't matter for the two that do. The real risk isn't being low-trust; it's being low-trust and unable to command attention.

Which reframes 2016's original error. The party put its thumb on the scale for the candidate farthest from the well — optimizing for institutional backing — over the one (Sanders, trust ≈0.22, a 40%-moveable coalition) sitting on it with demonstrable affect. Could Clinton have won anyway? Structurally, the ceiling was low. Within it, the margin was squandered on signaling (the Goldman optics, the email handling, skipping Wisconsin), not on policy — because policy was never the axis that decided these voters.

Figure 13 · Two strategies, two levers

Each cell is anchored to a real result: choice is decided by trust (Fig 7), turnout by affect and contact rather than policy (Fig 6B), and perceived trust is sticky (Fig 12). The establishment row loses both levers; the outsider row wins both. The lesson for 2028 isn't "go left" or "go center" — it's pick someone whose trust-position already matches the base you need.

So what?

"Move to the center" is a horizontal answer to a vertical problem

Put it together. "Move to the center to be electable" fails three ways at once: the center you're chasing barely exists; you can't move turnout with policy anyway; and the thing that actually wins — being trusted as a real, on-your-side outsider — can't be faked by triangulating. For years "electability" has been the club used to tell progressives to sit down and pick the safe option. The data says it's backwards: in a low-trust era, the safe establishment candidate is the risky one.

The longer version

Step back from 2016 — it was only the worked example. Underneath it is a geometry. The electorate's center of mass sits low on trust; candidates and the establishment cluster high on it — far, vertically, from where the voters actually are. Conventional strategy treats the contest as horizontal: slide left or right to reach "the center." That's the fallacy.

Horizontal motion can't close a vertical gap. Trust isn't something you buy by moving on policy — the two axes do different jobs (Figure 6), so a high-trust candidate stays far from the contestable mass no matter where they stand left-to-right. Political gravity pulls the up-for-grabs toward whoever sits closest on trust, not on policy. The question for 2028 isn't how far left or right — it's how far down.

  • Bernie → Trump, not Bernie → Hillary. On a 1D map, Hillary is between Bernie and Trump. In 2D trust-space, Trump and Bernie are in the same low-trust hemisphere. Hillary is not.
  • "Drain the swamp" is a trust message, not a policy message. It works because the target audience is the low-trust quadrant — and it's available to candidates on either side of the ideology axis.
  • Pelosi, Biden, Kamala, and Romney occupy the same quadrant. High trust, moderate ideology. Swing voters in the low-trust center feel no gravitational pull toward any of them — regardless of policy.
  • AOC, Sanders, Platner occupy the same quadrant as Trump. Not on ideology — on the institutional trust axis. That's where the disaffected voter mass lives. That's the contest.

Methodology & Sources

Voter data: ANES 2016 Time Series (Sep 2019 release, n=4,270; 3,301 with valid ideology + trust scores). Ideology: V161126 (7-point, rescaled −1 to +1, 99="haven't thought about it" treated as missing). Party ID: V161159 (5-point summary). Vote: V162034a.

Weighting: all densities and centroids apply the ANES pre-election survey weight (V160101, normalized to mean 1) — the raw sample is not self-weighting. Weights post-stratify to Census demographic benchmarks (age, sex, race, education, region), so they do not force party ID or vote to match the electorate; weighting shifts our numbers by <2 points. For the contested question of how non-voters lean, ANES is least reliable (vote over-report, thin non-voter subsample) — there we defer to external studies (e.g. Knight Foundation), which find non-voters split roughly evenly, not strongly Democratic.

Trust index (3-item systemic): mean of V161215 (trust government to do what is right), V161216 (run for benefit of all vs. a few big interests), and V161217 (whether government wastes tax money) — each recoded so higher = more trust and normalized 0–1. The three cohere (Cronbach α=0.59) and the index correlates r=−0.35 with Trump vote, stronger than any single item. 2016 trust was floor-heavy: population mean 0.22, median 0.17. An earlier single-item version (V161215 only) was both weaker and forced visible Likert banding; this index resolves both.

Why corruption is excluded. The ANES battery's corruption item (V161218, "how many running government are corrupt") runs opposite the three systemic items and positively predicts Trump vote — so averaging it in cancels signal (α collapses to 0.16). It taps a different, incumbent-relative dimension: measured pre-election under a Democratic administration, Clinton voters rated government more corrupt (0.55) than Trump voters did (0.43). "Corruption" in 2016 was a contested, bipartisan frame; Trump's anti-corruption appeal ran through systemic rigging ("drain the swamp"), captured by the three systemic items where his voters score lowest-trust — not through diffuse venality.

Caveat — trust is partly partisan. Institutional trust tracks who holds the White House (Hetherington 2005; Citrin & Stoker 2018): the in-party trusts more. The 2016 ANES was collected under Obama, which inflates Democratic and deflates Republican readings. The thesis rests on groups outside the in-party — independents and Sanders primary voters, who sit low regardless — and on within-2016 vote behavior.

Candidate positions: Ideology from DW-NOMINATE first dimension (voteview.com) where legislative record exists. Perceived ideology from ANES voter placement items (V161128 Clinton, V161129 Trump). Establishment posture constructed from endorsement patterns, DW-NOMINATE second dimension (tracks insider/outsider since ~2010), and campaign rhetoric. Estimated positions flagged with ⚠.

Key papers: Hetherington (2005) Why Trust Matters; Mutz (2018) "Status Threat, Not Economic Hardship" PNAS; Lenz (2012) Follow the Leader; Hutter & Kriesi (2019) on populist electoral dynamics; Enders & Uscinski (2021) "Primary Distrust" — directly tests institutional distrust as driver of both Trump and Sanders support.
