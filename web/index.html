The Center Doesn't Exist

Why "swing voters" aren't moderates — they're the disillusioned

The conventional story

Voters on a line

Political analysts love a simple left-right spectrum. Independents sit in the middle. To win them, you move to the center.

Figure 1 · 1D spectrum

On this view, "Independent" = "moderate." Winning them means triangulating toward the middle on policy.

A different lens

Add trust. The map changes.

What if we plot the same voters on two axes: left-right ideology and a 3-item index of trust in government — does it do what's right, run for everyone, spend money wisely? Data from the 2016 American National Election Study (n=3,301).

Figure 2 · Ideology × trust

The "center" empties out. Most voters who look moderate on policy actually sit in the low-trust quadrant — not the moderate quadrant.

From the crowd to the candidates

Within one primary, trust sorts the candidates.

The same party blobs as Figure 2 — Democrats blue, Independents purple, Republicans red — with a marker for where each candidate's voters center. Inside the Democratic primary, Clinton's and Sanders' voters split on trust: Sanders' sit lower on both the mean (0.32 → 0.22) and the median (0.25 → 0.17). Trust, not ideology, is what separated them.

Figure 2B · Crowd → candidates

Trust is skewed off the floor, so the mean (hollow ring) floats above each blob while the median (filled dot) sits inside it. Toggle between them — Sanders stays below Clinton either way.

The punchline

Bernie voters and Trump voters are both low-trust. Hillary's aren't.

Using ANES pre-election primary vote reports: where do each candidate's own voters actually sit in ideology × trust space? The dots mark each group's centroid. The blobs show their distribution.

Figure 3 · Primary vs general

Clinton's voters are the high-trust bloc (primary 0.32, general 0.28). Sanders' primary voters (0.22) sit well below them, down in the low-trust band toward Trump's voters (general 0.14) — between the two, but on the distrustful side of the Clinton wall. The Bernie→Trump switchers were near-centrist on ideology (x≈0) and bottom of the trust axis. On a 1D map Hillary sits between Bernie and Trump; on the trust axis she's the high-trust outlier.

Official vs. perceived

Where candidates stand — and where voters think they stand

Solid dots = where voters perceived the candidate (ANES ideology placement, rescaled). Hollow dots = official policy position (DW-NOMINATE legislative voting record). The connecting line is the perception gap.

Clinton and Biden were among the most progressive Democrats on paper. But voters placed them firmly in the high-institutional-trust, establishment quadrant. Meanwhile, Clinton's ANES "honest" trait score (3.95/5) was worse than Trump's (3.72/5) — the Goldman Sachs problem, in the data.

Political gravity

Whose coalition overlapped with the contestable mass?

The terrain is the moveable voter mass — undecided and disengaged voters, the only people whose vote is actually up for grabs (n=1,028). The dashed blobs are each candidate's primary coalition. The question is the overlap: whose supporters sat inside the contestable zone?

Sanders' primary coalition was 40% moveable — three times Clinton's 13%. His blob sat directly on top of the contestable mass. Clinton's sat above it in the high-trust zone. When Sanders wasn't on the general ballot, that overlap had nowhere obvious to go.

Figure 5 · Coalition overlap

Among Sanders' 339 primary voters: 62% voted Clinton, 21% didn't vote, 7% voted Trump. The Bernie→Trump voters had near-centrist ideology (x ≈ 0) — they didn't move right on policy. They moved on trust.

Figure 6 · Sanders redistribution

Research basis: Lenz (2012) Follow the Leader documents that voters adopt the positions of their favored candidate over time as identity function — not the other way around. Proximity in 2D predicts who they follow.

So what?

Moving to the "center" is the wrong map

If the dominant axis for swing voters is trust vs. distrust — not left vs. right — then moderating policy positions to appeal to the center is solving the wrong problem.

  • Bernie → Trump, not Bernie → Hillary. On a 1D map, Hillary is between Bernie and Trump. In 2D trust-space, Trump and Bernie are in the same low-trust hemisphere. Hillary is not.
  • "Drain the swamp" is a trust message, not a policy message. It works because the target audience is the low-trust quadrant — and it's available to candidates on either side of the ideology axis.
  • Pelosi, Biden, Kamala, and Romney occupy the same quadrant. High trust, moderate ideology. Swing voters in the low-trust center feel no gravitational pull toward any of them — regardless of policy.
  • AOC, Sanders, Platner occupy the same quadrant as Trump. Not on ideology — on the institutional trust axis. That's where the disaffected voter mass lives. That's the contest.

Methodology & Sources

Voter data: ANES 2016 Time Series (Sep 2019 release, n=4,270; 3,301 with valid ideology + trust scores). Ideology: V161126 (7-point, rescaled −1 to +1, 99="haven't thought about it" treated as missing). Party ID: V161159 (5-point summary). Vote: V162034a.

Trust index (3-item systemic): mean of V161215 (trust government to do what is right), V161216 (run for benefit of all vs. a few big interests), and V161217 (whether government wastes tax money) — each recoded so higher = more trust and normalized 0–1. The three cohere (Cronbach α=0.59) and the index correlates r=−0.35 with Trump vote, stronger than any single item. 2016 trust was floor-heavy: population mean 0.22, median 0.17. An earlier single-item version (V161215 only) was both weaker and forced visible Likert banding; this index resolves both.

Why corruption is excluded. The ANES battery's corruption item (V161218, "how many running government are corrupt") runs opposite the three systemic items and positively predicts Trump vote — so averaging it in cancels signal (α collapses to 0.16). It taps a different, incumbent-relative dimension: measured pre-election under a Democratic administration, Clinton voters rated government more corrupt (0.55) than Trump voters did (0.43). "Corruption" in 2016 was a contested, bipartisan frame; Trump's anti-corruption appeal ran through systemic rigging ("drain the swamp"), captured by the three systemic items where his voters score lowest-trust — not through diffuse venality.

Caveat — trust is partly partisan. Institutional trust tracks who holds the White House (Hetherington 2005; Citrin & Stoker 2018): the in-party trusts more. The 2016 ANES was collected under Obama, which inflates Democratic and deflates Republican readings. The thesis rests on groups outside the in-party — independents and Sanders primary voters, who sit low regardless — and on within-2016 vote behavior.

Candidate positions: Ideology from DW-NOMINATE first dimension (voteview.com) where legislative record exists. Perceived ideology from ANES voter placement items (V161128 Clinton, V161129 Trump). Establishment posture constructed from endorsement patterns, DW-NOMINATE second dimension (tracks insider/outsider since ~2010), and campaign rhetoric. Estimated positions flagged with ⚠.

Key papers: Hetherington (2005) Why Trust Matters; Mutz (2018) "Status Threat, Not Economic Hardship" PNAS; Lenz (2012) Follow the Leader; Hutter & Kriesi (2019) on populist electoral dynamics; Enders & Uscinski (2021) "Primary Distrust" — directly tests institutional distrust as driver of both Trump and Sanders support.
