The Center Is a Lie

How chasing “electability” became American politics’ surest way to lose.

By James Villarrubia · June 30, 2026

The argument, start to finish

1 The era. Almost no one trusts the government anymore. In 1964 roughly three in four Americans said they trusted Washington to do what is right most of the time; today barely one in five do, a sixty-year collapse that spared no party and no generation. That quiet withdrawal of faith in our institutions, more than any single policy fight, is the climate every modern election is actually run in.

2 Which middle? Into that climate the pundits offer one perennial cure, move to the center, but that advice rests on a group that barely exists. “Persuadable,” “moderate,” “centrist,” and “swing” are terms that get used as if they mean the same people. Instead, they describe four different groups, and the so-called "courtable moderate middle," i.e. the voter sitting patiently between the parties waiting to be wooed by a more reasonable platform, is really only a sliver of the populace. The voters who actually decide elections, the ones who switch and the ones who stay home, are not moderates.

3 Who’s in play. Those decisive voters do share something though: they almost all skew low-trust. Swing voters don’t reward the most centrist policy mix; they reward the candidate who fits the moment, who reads as a break from a system they have stopped believing in. Non-voters aren’t holding out for a gentler agenda or a more likeable face either; they show up when the stakes feel real and the candidate fits the moment. In a low-trust era the currency is authenticity and regime-fit, not policy position.

4 Why insiders lose. This is the test the Democratic Party keeps failing. They nominate insiders: the reassuring, well-credentialed, establishment candidate. But in an era defined by distrust of the establishment that pick reads as the wrong answer to the only question voters are asking. Tacking to the center doesn’t rescue it because courtable voters aren't in the center. So a center pivot gets no voters while also signaling a candidate beholden to polling and insider control. The low-trust voters who might have been persuaded are instead pushed away.

5 The payoff. So our conclusion inverts the conventional wisdom. In an era of low-trust, the “electable centrist" and the "safe DC-insider" are the risky bets. The candidate cast as the outsider, the one fit for a "change era," is the one positioned to win. Electability isn’t a fixed personal trait you can audition for by sounding moderate; it’s a match between a candidate and a moment. And the moment is now, almost permanently, a low-trust one. Winning doesn’t mean crowding the center, but instead owning the outsider lane.

Part I

The myth of the center

Why “move to the center” fails.

§0 · The era

The trust collapse

As the country marks its 250th anniversary, celebrating the world’s longest-enduring constitution and longest continuously operating democratic republic, what should feel like a moment of national pride instead feels oddly subdued. Amongst the ceremonies and spectacle, there are certainly political firsts: the White House staged a UFC fight on the South Lawn.1 And, of course, there is also more standard pageantry: the National Mall is holding a dutiful but thinly attended Great American State Fair.2 But that thin attendance is somehow not unexpected, given our political moment. Even in our nation's capital, there seems to be an inability to muster much energy for civic joy.

For many Americans, the accumulated mental load of parsing the near daily institutional disruptions has produced something closer to exhaustion than rage. As the 4th approaches, the Supreme Court has just upheld birthright citizenship itself,3 a right so core to the American experience that legal scholars are unnerved to find it a live question at all. But it held only 6–3, and it should have been unanimous: a ruling the other way would have fundamentally altered major precedent, long-standing practice, and the very intent of the Amendment's drafters. And perhaps that is the problem: it's not that these questions loom. It is that the looming questions are all so momentous that we can no longer trust what came before to guide what comes next. We are institutionally adrift. Will the President undo another enduring constitutional norm? Will the Supreme Court ignore stare decisis and unwind a long-held understanding of a civil right? Will Congress cede more of its power to the executive in its choice to do nothing?

This undercurrent of political unrest is not without precedent. It recalls the America of the 1820s, when national power was still unclear, the federal government's authority was openly contested, and Andrew Jackson rose on a populist surge because the country had not yet decided what or who our democracy was meant to represent. That uncertainty makes sense for a young republic finding its footing. But it is odd at the quarter-millennial celebration, after decades of the post-war American dream, after a checkered rise to global military and economic power, after a storied run of middle-class growth and steady upward advance. Something has quietly gone wrong. Something has sapped our "unifying patriotism." A 2023 Pew study found that 65% of Americans said they always or often feel exhausted when thinking about politics.4 And that weariness is decidedly non-partisan: shared across age, race, and political commitment. Only 2% described American politics in positive terms. Just two percent.4

Maybe the explanation is simpler than we want to believe: that almost no one trusts the government anymore. In 1964, roughly three in four Americans said they trusted Washington to do what is right most of the time;5 today barely one in five do,6 a collapse in trust that has spared no party and no generation and took the better part of sixty years to complete.7 At this point, the collapse in trust is less a growing grievance than a final verdict, a quiet withdrawal of faith in the American Experiment.

Everything this essay argues about the center, about who wins elections, and about why safe, electable candidates are so often the ones who lose, begins in that quiet withdrawal.

A quick note on the data before we begin our argument in earnest. Almost everything that follows draws on polling and survey data from presidential elections, and that is a deliberate choice for three reasons. First, the presidential-year data is simply more complete, with larger samples and deeper question batteries, which is what lets us surface the more nuanced statistical findings the rest of this analysis leans on. Second, presidential races set the terms of the conversation for almost every down-ballot contest, so the forces that decide them tend to shape House, Senate, and state races as well. Third, the steady consolidation of power in the executive branch in recent years, pushed by President Trump and ratified by a deferential Supreme Court, has made the presidency more consequential than ever. Thus, understanding how the presidency is won is a very useful starting place for understanding the undercurrents of American political belief.

§1 · The conventional wisdom

Which middle?

Despite the growing ocean of distrust in government, almost every campaign consultant believes the same thing: there's large bloc of gettable, moderate voters sitting in the middle, and if you soften your positions to win them over, you win the election. "Don't scare the moderates," the advice goes. "Move to the center." After Kamala Harris's 2024 loss, Rahm Emanuel, Obama’s former chief of staff and potential 2028 contender, told the Wall Street Journal that the Democratic brand had gone “weak and woke” and the only way back was "the center." A whole post-election industry agreed. Centrist groups like Third Way built campaigns around the same diagnosis — that the party drifted too far left and the road home runs through the middle. The advice is older than any one cycle, going back to Bill Clinton’s triangulation, and it has rarely been louder than right now. And for all the repetition, almost nobody stops to check whether it’s true.8 So we checked.

Turns out moderate, centrist, and swing voter aren't even the same group of people.9 But the strategy textbooks treat them like one bloc.

§2 · The center is a lie

One voter in a hundred

If swing, moderate, and centrist are different groups, where does the confusion come from? Well, the electability pitch imagines one specific prize: real voters who hold moderate views across the actual issues who are just waiting for a moderate candidate to support. But do those voters even exist? We can find them by funneling down our data sets of voter preferences. The funnel is simple: start with the whole electorate, then filter it down step by step, from everyone, down to the people who call themselves moderate, and finally to the people whose positions are actually moderate. Walk it honestly, and that courtable bloc all but vanishes.

Start with 100 American voters. 26 tell pollsters they haven’t thought about ideology at all, so they’re not in the funnel. Of the remaining 74, about 40 self-place as moderate. Push them on real issues and 17 turn out to be Grab-Baggers (self-described moderates whose actual policy positions are lopsided, with an issue average that lands clearly off-center), 23 are MidMiddlers (voters whose issue positions only average to the middle), and just 1 is a True Middler (centrist on a majority of issues, not just on average).10

Even the 23 MidMiddlers aren’t a coherent target, because the average of their issues’ left-right scoring only lands near the center by accident: strong-left positions on some issues cancel strong-right ones on others. Thus a "shift to the center on policy X" reaches a different MidMiddler with every X you pick. There’s no shared platform that gets all 23 of them at once because their centrism is a statistical artifact of opposite extremes rather than a position on the issue map.

The mirage is also bigger than even the moderate label suggests. If you drop the requirement that a voter call themselves moderate and simply ask who lands near the center once you average their six issue positions, the pool swells to more than a third of the whole electorate, roughly 38%. And most of that 38% are self-described liberals and conservatives whose opposite-leaning answers happen to average into a middling score.10 That is the averaging illusion at full size: a third of all voters can look centrist on average while almost none of them actually hold strong centrist positions across the board.

The only voter for whom a single centrist agenda cohesively lands is the True Middler, who is centrist on every issue at the same time, and that voter is 1 in 100.

That’s the courtable middle the textbook actually describes: one voter, surrounded by 99 who are either disengaged, partisan, or wearing the moderate label over policy preferences that pull hard in one direction. The lie isn’t that moderates exist, and it isn’t that voters never shift. The lie is that "the center" is a coherent place a candidate can run toward.

§3 · So who actually swings? (almost nobody)

Two blocs in disguise

By walking the funnel by policy position, we show that the coherent centrists are only a very small sliver of the voting populace. But position is not behavior. How do these voters behave in elections, not just once, but across cycles? Does a candidate's policy shift move their vote at all?

It turns out that behavior does not align with self-labeling. Calling yourself a moderate doesn't actually predict that you vote like one. Most self-described moderates show up every cycle and vote for the same party they always have, no different from the people who call themselves liberal or conservative. Look at one election and the middle looks up for grabs. Look at three in a row and it isn’t.11

What the "moderate" label hides is a near-even split between two committed partisan blocs. And they are not a courtable middle. The "moderate center" is more akin to a tie zone where two consistent partisan groups happen to use the same self-label: moderate. There are plenty of reasons people land on that word without actually being moderate or centrist. In a highly partisan community or locality, adopting the outsider party’s label can be socially costly: friction with neighbors, friction at work, friction in your kids’ school community. In an era of unusually charged partisanship, "moderate" is safe cover. It signals nothing to anyone and offends no one. The label doesn’t make a person a swing voter; it makes them a voter evaluating the same things as everyone else.

If put aside the "moderate label and look at behaviors alone, our target swing voters add up to roughly 8–9% of the electorate; and, most importantly they’re spread across the ideology spectrum rather than concentrated in the middle.12

What does that leave us with? Who are the "gettable" voters? Well, they are a genuinely small group defined by behavior across cycles rather than by self-label or policy position. They either switch, or they don’t reliably show up. From here on, when we say "the voters in play," we mean exactly that group: the switchers and the no-shows. Two completely different problems, distributed across the spectrum, not concentrated in the middle.

Part II

Winning is hard

Voters don’t act the way campaigns think they act.

§4 · Two completely different jobs

Choice and turnout

To recap, moderates and centrists are not reliably defined by policy or label. Those labels do little to reflect voters in play, who span the whole spectrum of ideology. We instead, identified the voters in play, the switchers and the no-shows, by their behaviors. They are not a big, juicy, courtable bloc of voters, but rather a small minority of the electorate. Switchers are roughly 1 in 10, and no-shows are a separate group of similar size.14 And unfortunately for campaign strategists, those two groups represent two completely separate problems for a campaign, and they don’t respond to the same incentives or actions.15

Take the switchers first. They don’t sit in any one place ideologically. The voters who change parties between cycles span the whole spectrum, from Obama-then-Trump voters in 2016 to Trump-then-Biden voters in 2020. What links them isn’t a shared policy position. It’s a pattern of being unattached enough to occasionally move.

The no-shows look different. Most have a clear partisan lean and could tell you in one sentence which side they’re on. What they don’t have is enough reason to make the trip to the polls. Some have given up on the system delivering anything. Some don’t think their vote will matter either way. They aren’t undecided about who, they’re undecided about whether to vote.

§5 · Buried Assumptions

The grid’s three blind spots

Campaigns already evaluate and organize potential voters by the two problems we just named. For every name on the voter file they ask two questions: which side does this person lean? (Democrat, persuadable, Republican) and how reliably do they show up to vote? (high, mid, low propensity). Cross those two questions and you get nine groups, the classic 9-box grid, and each box gets a mechanical strategy campaigns have relied on for decades.16

The 9-box grid is, undoubtedly, useful. The trouble isn’t with the cross-tab itself; it’s three assumptions its layout quietly invites a campaign to make, each of which turns out wrong:

  1. Assumption 1: the “persuadable” voter sits in the political middle. We've already shown that our gettable voters are not ideologically "in the middle." But the grid puts persuadable in the middle column, between Lean D on the left and Lean R on the right. The layout implies a moderate, between-the-extremes voter. The catch is that the columns run on a partisan axis, the probability a voter breaks your way, not an ideological one, the probability a voter agrees with your platform positions. Those two things are related, but not as closely as you'd think. Ideologically, the gettable voters genuinely span the whole spectrum: left, right, and center. Reading the partisan middle as an ideological middle is what makes “move to the center” feel like the natural strategy for winning them. If persuadables aren’t actually in the ideological middle, then moving toward the middle isn’t moving towards them. Persuadable is not moderate is not centrist is not swing.
  2. Assumption 2: voters who lean your way but don’t show up are loyal but lazy. Often, they’re not. They’re disaffected. They don’t vote because they already align with your policy but still don’t trust your candidate. A louder push from that candidate can make their defection more likely. Thus the grid sees who doesn’t show up, not why, and that blind spot has a measurable cost: a campaign that follows the grid can spend real money on door-knocks that produce votes for the opponent.17
  3. Assumption 3: persuasion and turnout are independent moves. They aren’t, and laying them out in separate columns is what makes the interaction easy to miss. Campaign staffers rarely stop to ask “what does this persuasion move cost me on the turnout side?” A “safe, electable” pitch designed to win persuadables can simultaneously dampen turnout among disaffected supporters who needed a reason to show up. The trade-off is real, and we measure it directly in §18; the grid just hides it.18

The low-propensity lean-D cell carries flag 2 for the hidden defection risk. The low-propensity persuadable cell, already the hardest box to make progress in, carries flag 3 for the interaction between persuasion and turnout moves.

Zoom in on flag 2, the assumption with the most measurable cost. The grid only works on what campaigns typically have access to — a voter file’s “lean-Dem” label but not the disaffection underneath it. The label combines voter history, demographics, and survey-modeled partisanship, but it normally doesn’t carry a trust dimension. So when the grid tells a campaign to mobilize a low-propensity lean-D voter, that voter may already trust the system enough to come back home, or they may have stopped trusting it years ago and be one push from defecting. The grid can’t tell the difference. Figure I shows what that costs.

Pause and really consider what Figure I is showing. Misjudging the trust or grievance level of your low-propensity lean-Dem voters can roughly quintuple the rate at which a Get-Out-The-Vote (GOTV) door-knock or text returns a vote for the opponent. The voter file’s label says the cell is yours. The trust composite says a meaningful fraction of it isn’t. That is a massive blind spot, and it sits in the cell campaigns spend the most money on.

While 2's defection risk is the easiest to put a clear number on, it’s only one of three buried assumptions. 1 and 3 carry similar problems. To see all three risks more clearly, we have to split the grid back into its underlying framing questions, choice and turnout, and look at each on its own terms. That’s where the next two parts of the analysis go: choice in Part III, turnout in Part IV. Both test the central question that underlies the 9-box grid: What actions, if any, can a campaign take that provably move voters towards your candidate?

Part III

The choice

What drives who you pick.

§6 · By the time it’s two famous names, the race is mostly set

Persuasion: a measured zero

We started our analysis with a refutation of the pundit class's "tack to the middle" strategy. But the timing of this is as critical as the move itself. Typically, candidates take more partisan positions in the primary and then "tack" after the nomination and before the general election. That's the classic strategy at least. But that advice implicitly assumes that voters’ minds are still open to change after a primary season. Are they? Once both candidates are nationally known, do their policy shifts actually change how people vote?

This has been tested and tested again, across 49 field experiments. Between two well-known candidates, all the ads and door-knocks combined move vote choice by roughly zero.20 Once you’ve got two famous nominees, the election runs on who they already are rather than what they say in the last stretch,21 and the decision that mattered was made earlier, at the nomination.22

One important scope note before we move on, because two things are easy to confuse. The claim above is about persuasion: late-campaign contact does not change which candidate someone picks when both nominees are already famous. It doesn’t mean contact does nothing at all. Contact still moves turnout, sometimes by several percentage points, and we come back to turnout in §12. Choice and turnout are different outcomes, and the same channels affect them differently. We need to keep them separated.

§7 · So what makes a reachable voter bolt?

Distrust, inside your own tent

If choice doesn’t depend on policy positions, then what makes a swing voter swing? The obvious guess in a low-trust era is distrust: the disaffected should be the ones who bolt. For clarity, we’ll call voters with low institutional trust System Critics and those with high trust System Believers.23

At first the data says no — trust is not the driver of swing voter behavior. Across the whole electorate, System Critics swing no more often than System Believers do. But like "centrist voter," the trust analysis is a statistical mirage of averages. The signal hides because the most distrustful single bloc in any given cycle is usually the base of the out-of-power party, loyally voting for its own outsider rather than swinging. For example, in 2016 that bloc was Republicans voting happily for Trump, their own anti-establishment candidate. When we pull out each party's respective base, the trust measurement snaps into focus: among non-Republicans (Dems + indeps), System Critics defected at 53% and System Believers at just 10%. On the GOP side the pattern flipped: distrust there meant staying loyal to Trump.24 System Critics more reliably vote for the out-of-power party, which in turn induces the swing behavior.

There is one important caveat about scope. This data is from presidential campaigns. The survey's trust metrics are thus spread across the national electorate. Many local races, and plenty of congressional ones, are not like that: geographic sorting can leave a district that runs almost entirely System Critic or almost entirely System Believer. The specific finding here will not hold at every level uniformly. What does carry across levels is the instruction underneath it: you have to understand the trust dimension inside your own ecosystem, because it is likely an axis your particular race turns on.

§8 · "Authenticity" depends on who’s looking

Same candidates, opposite reads

The last section showed distrust pushes voters toward the outsider or out-of-power party, and can induce swing behavior because of changes in party power. But that raises a sharper question: why does the very same candidate read as honest to a System Critic and phony to a System Believer? What our analysis suggests is that whether a candidate seems honest, or seems to care about people like you, was never a fixed fact about the candidate. It depends on who’s doing the looking.25

In 2016, System Critics saw Trump as the honest one and Clinton as the phony. System Believers saw the exact opposite. Same two candidates, judgment completely flipped.26 This means you don’t get "authentic" by being authentic in the abstract. You get it by being close to where the voter already stands on system trust. Alignment between voter and candidate is what reads as real.

§10 · Why a candidate can flip-flop for free, or get punished for it

The same flip, two verdicts

Given our analysis so far, we can posit that authenticity runs on alignment between voter and candidate of their institutional trust, not on alignment of policy. That has a strange consequence, and it cuts both ways. Start with the upside: a candidate that voters read as one of their own can actually move their positions for them. That’s why Trump could reverse Republican positions overnight and his voters just followed. Researchers tested it directly: tell Republicans "Trump backs this liberal idea," and they’ll back it too, even against their own stated beliefs.29 When voters trust you as one of them, your exact policies barely matter.

The downside is then that for candidates lacking in trust, no amount of policy pivots will save their campaigns. And even worse, such pivots can exacerbate your trust gap. When they think you’re a calculating insider (the way System Critics saw Clinton), the same flip looks like a cynical move. Trump rewrites his platform and it’s "telling it like it is." Clinton shifts left to match Bernie and it’s "she’ll say anything." Same act, opposite reaction.30 Trust, not policy.

§11 · So what actually flips a switcher?

Status, not policy

Up to now we’ve shown what doesn’t flip a swing voter: not the policy platform, not a clever move to the center. So what does flip a swing voter? To answer that, we would have to measure the same people before and after an election. The Democracy Fund VOTER Survey is a panel that did exactly that in 2011, four years before the Obama→Trump switch.31

The survey confirmed that voters who went from Obama in 2012 to Trump in 2016 didn’t switch on policy, but surprisingly the survey suggests they also didn’t directly switch on distrust in government. What moved them was a sense of lost status with a racial edge, the feeling that "the system now puts other groups ahead of people like me." Obama’s election stirred this up, and Trump’s campaign used it to its advantage.32 We hold this hypothesis of racial status loss loosely, on purpose. It does not mean half the country is simply racist. It means racial status anxiety is the dividing line that grew strongest over this period, the one that increasingly sorted who voted for whom.

The model is straightforward. Hold ideology, party ID, and economic outlook constant, and see what predicts the switch. The chart below is the result: the racial-resentment items measured four years before the vote carry roughly two-thirds the weight of ideology itself, and they dwarf every other predictor on the left-hand side. The one that looks surprisingly small is distrust.

At first glance, that small distrust bar seems like it contradicts all of our analysis so far. It does not. Distrust hasn’t stopped mattering, it’s stopped discriminating. In 2016, 71% of all voters scored in the bottom quarter of the trust index and 45% sat at the absolute floor.33 A variable that almost everyone shares can’t tell you which of them switched. Racial resentment varies across voters, so it can discriminate. Distrust is now so universal that it can’t reliably predict individual votes. It still has an impact, however.

You might wonder if 2016 was just a one-off, or is the racial-resentment impact on voting a structural component now? Take the same model to the ANES Cumulative File and rerun it across data from every available presidential cycle from 1988 to 2024. The trend is hard to miss.

Clearly the trend for racial resentment is robust. But we haven't connected how "lost status," a kind of immiseration measured in standing rather than wages,34 becomes a vote preference? The argument doesn’t run on raw racism alone, and saying it does would be both wrong and politically useless. There is a chain of psychological mechanisms involved, and the psych literature converges on three of them in particular. And this is where our ocean of distrust comes back into the picture.

The first psychological mechanism is that status threat beats economic anxiety. Diana Mutz, in a 2018 PNAS study using the same kind of before-and-after panel, tested whether Trump support was driven by actual economic loss or by perceived loss of group status. Status threat won, by a lot. Sides, Tesler, and Vavreck’s Identity Crisis and Tesler’s Post-Racial or Most-Racial say the same thing: Obama’s election didn’t create racial resentment, it activated and racialized what was already there.35

The second is that people reach for simpler stories when they feel threatened. The integrative-complexity literature, going back to Tetlock in the 1970s and 1980s, shows that threatened voters switch from differentiated, conditional reasoning to categorical, single-cause attributions. "They took our jobs" is a one-clause explanation. "Automation plus offshoring plus the college wage premium plus the long decline of unionized manufacturing" is the actual story, but you can’t tell that one at a rally, and it doesn’t fit on a hat.36

The third is that frustration finds the available villain. Realistic group conflict theory (Sherif’s Robbers Cave studies, LeVine and Campbell’s Ethnocentrism) and decades of follow-on work all show that frustration displaces onto safer out-groups. When a fifty-year-old man loses a stable factory job in Ohio, the cause is largely structural. But the villain who is actually within reach is the immigrant down the road, or the social program he believes is favoring somebody else.37

So the resulting racial lens isn’t an accident. It is a chain reaction. Lost status is the fuel, simpler stories are what people reach for under threat, and the nearest available villain is the one who gets blamed. Stack those three together and you get the chain below.

None of which means voters are simply racist. The status loss is real, but the racial frame is just the nearest target for it. Trump didn’t invent either one, he leveraged them.

Go back to the factory worker in Ohio. The feeling that the system now serves other people and not him is itself a kind of institutional distrust, the same distrust this essay has tracked all along. A voter who feels that severe of a status loss is not waiting for anyone to shave the corners off a platform. He leans toward whoever stands plainly against the establishment, and leans away from anyone who responds to his anger by sounding more reasonable, more electable, more like the establishment itself. The language of the supposed “center” has always been a language of moderation, and moderation feels inadequate to the problems he sees, in the country and in his own standing. He waited a long time for the system to be fixed, and at some point along the way he stopped believing it was broken. He has decided it is working exactly as it was designed to work, and that it was never designed for him.

That tells you something about what a campaign can and can’t do. If a swing voter’s read of a candidate is filtered through an already-set lens about whose side the system is on, then the persuasion levers a campaign normally reaches for, like sharper messaging, more polished policy, or a move to the middle, barely move the dial. They’re moves inside a frame the voter has already locked in. This is exactly what the field experiments on late-campaign persuasion showed: across 49 of them, between two famous nominees, the effect on vote choice was zero.

Which leaves a stark strategic implication. Swing voters are a headwind in the general election, not a sale opportunity. You don’t win these voters by sliding left or right. The only window where a campaign can shape how a candidate is perceived is the one where the candidate is still being formed, before the lens hardens. By the time candidates are nominated, the window to swing voters has usually closed.

Part IV

The turnout

What decides whether you show up.

§12 · You can’t buy turnout with policy

Contact, not content

Now, to the other half of our two campaign problems: turnout, the question of whether you show up to vote at all. Our look at late-campaign persuasion found you can’t move vote choice between two already-famous nominees just by talking at them. But turnout is a separate outcome, driven by separate things. What we want to understand is this: outside of the choice of candidate, can a campaign actually increase turnout? And is a better policy platform, or a more centrist one, the thing that does it? Well, we don’t have to guess. Campaigns have run real experiments for decades: knock on this door, mail this flyer, see who shows up.38

The answer is as dismissive of policy pivots as the voter choice. Policy pivots do not work. What gets people to vote is being contacted and feeling something: a knock, a neighbor, or really just a reason to care. A mailer explaining your health-care plan does basically nothing for turnout. A postcard telling you your neighbors are watching does a lot.39 You don’t talk people into voting with policy; you give them a reason to show up.

§13 · It’s stakes, not affection

Caring, not liking

But contact must still carry some sort of message, ideally one that improves turnout. We saw one important piece of evidence back in our discussion of the social-pressure mail experiment in §12, where a postcard telling voters their neighbors were watching raised turnout by about 8 points in randomized trials. From this research we know a stakes-flavored message can raise turnout. But "stakes" and "social pressure" is still pretty broad. To see better understand turnout messaging, we have to look at what kind of "stakes" correlates with the act of voting in the first place.

The 2016 ANES measures stakes with a single question: does the voter "care a good deal who wins"? Voters who say yes turn out at about 71%; those who say no, at about 36%. That 35-point gap is the single biggest bivariate predictor of turnout in the file.40

That can sound circular: of course people who say they care about an election are more likely to vote in it. The cleaner test is to look only at voters who aren’t political junkies to start with, the gettable ones any GOTV operation is trying to mobilize. Even among that group, voters who care turn out at 52% and voters who don’t turn out at 26%.41 So "caring" isn’t just a stand-in for general political engagement. It has its own pull on whether someone shows up.

You might guess that this "caring" comes from loving your own candidate. That’s certainly what most campaigns spend their budget on: warming voters up to the nominee, building identification, giving them somebody to root for. But that intuition doesn’t survive a deeper analysis of the data.

Run a multivariate model on what predicts turnout, holding everything else constant, and a different picture emerges. Caring who wins still dominates. Demographics (age, education) carry their share. Campaign contact, the lever from §12, registers solidly. And having strong feelings on both sides of the race adds a real lift. But the one variable that’s supposed to be the engine — warmth to your own candidate — comes out to essentially zero and possibly slightly negative. Voters who love their candidate more are not noticeably more likely to show up than voters who just kind of like them.42 What about "hating the other side"? Fear is typically a strong motivator and the "fear of the other side" hypothesis is suggestive in the 2016 data. But when we look across multiple cycles, the hypothesis doesn't hold. It's not the real answer either.

Luckily we do have data on what message content actually moves turnout. The GOTV field-experiment literature has run randomized trials on specific message types, and three of them produce real, replicated lifts:

  • Social pressure. Mailers showing voters their own and their neighbors’ public voting records lift turnout by about 8 points in randomized trials. This is the same Gerber-Green-Larimer result that already anchored §12.44
  • Voter-identity framing. Asking voters about "being a voter" (a noun, an identity) rather than about "voting" (a verb, an act) raised turnout by roughly 11 points across two pre-registered experiments. Bryan, Walton, Rogers and Dweck (2011, PNAS).45
  • Plan-to-vote prompts. Asking voters to specify when they will vote, where they will go, and how they will get there added about 4 points in their own randomized field experiment. Nickerson and Rogers (2010, Psychological Science).46

None of these promise the voter anything about policy. None of them depend on making the voter love the candidate. They each work because they make voting itself feel like something the voter is already committed to: an identity, a plan, a thing the neighbors are watching for.

Let's summarize our turnout analysis so far. When it comes to turnout:

  • Contact works.
  • Caring works.
  • Social pressure works.
  • Voter identity works.
  • Likeability does not.
  • Opponent dislike is unclear.
  • Policy does not.
Yet again, we can confirm that the pivot-to-the-center strategy is not effective in increasing turnout, just as it was not effective in changing voter choice. The turnout efforts that do pay off goes into contact, into making the election feel like it actually reaches voters, and into the three validated message types listed above. Whether there’s a sharper "fear of the other side" lever sitting on top of all that is something only the next round of field experiments will tell us. We just don’t know that yet.

§14 · …and the no-shows quit two different ways

Complacent vs alienated

Up to now, we've grouped the voters in need of turnout as one singular bloc. But our hypotheses are predicated on the impact of institutional trust. If we divide our turnout voters along our System Critic and System Believer lines, then two new subgroups emerge, each with a totally different and opposing lenses on "stakes" - creating yet another messaging conflict that campaigns must account for. 47

The first type is the complacent no-show: a System Believer with high institutional trust who skips the vote because nothing feels at stake. "Things are basically fine. Does it really matter who wins?" The second type is the alienated no-show: a System Critic who has stopped believing voting changes anything. "I care. I just don’t think my one vote fixes a rigged system." Same outcome on Election Day, two completely different reasons for it.

That shift, a 15% drop in complacency and 30% increase in alienation, is the whole turnout problem changing shape underneath the campaign infrastructure. What it means for our analysis is that turning out these voters isn’t one move, it’s two. The complacent ones, the System Believers who don’t care who wins, need a reason to care, as we just discussed above. Those voters are where most of the turnout research has focused: what stakes, what pressures make someone care enough to vote. On the other hand, the addition of a trust-dimension in our data shows us that, in fact, the vast majority of low-engagement voters are "alienated," System Critics who’ve given up, who need to believe their vote still counts. They might already care; they’ve just lost faith that anyone’s listening.

And this growing sense of alienation for low-engagement voters isn’t a transient issue of one election cycle. It’s a systemic issue that has been building for years, in parallel with or even because of the broader collapse in trust.

Put yourself in the shoes of one of these disaffected voters. You have been told, over and over, that your vote “matters.” Or in the Democratic case, that “Trump is the greatest threat,” or “the stakes have never been higher.” Those stakes-oriented messages now land flat with you, not because they aren’t believed, but because they are believed. Voters like you feel the daily impact of a system that doesn’t work for them. They feel it at the gas pump, at the grocery store, at the ATM. They don’t need further convincing of the stakes. The message has been absorbed. In 2020 we had the highest turnout in over a century amidst a tumultuous presidency and a global pandemic. The stakes were high! But in the years since, has status, perceived or real, gotten markedly better for you, the low-turnout, low-trust voter? Each side has had their chance at the helm: under Trump and Biden and Trump again. Are you better off now? Biden even said the ugly part out loud, inelegantly, during his own campaign: “nothing would fundamentally change” under his presidency. But change was nevertheless promised. Voters delivered turnout. And, if anything, the world is worse for many. Who would continue to trust that voting does anything?48

§15 · "That was just 2016"? No, it’s every election

Trust flips with the White House

We've now examined both halves of the campaign problem, the choice side and the turnout side. And both have kept pointing us back to the same underlying electoral force: a low-trust electorate that rewards outsiders and punishes the establishment. For simplicity, we’ve leaned heavily on 2016 to show these trends, partly because that cycle surprised so many, pollsters included, and partly because that election was a notable shift in trust. Our working thesis is that 2016 was the lowest-trust-era election to that point, with two of the first major outsider candidates in modern history. Trump and Sander's norm-defying primary campaigns were the first to show really how brittle our prior voter models and electoral assumptions were.

Given our reliance on 2016, as skeptic could easily dismiss our the theories as "was a Trump thing," or "a 2016 thing." §16 · The puzzle this solves

Stuck being the system

If institutional trust is truly an all-powerful predictor for our current low-trust era, then there’s still a puzzle our thesis hasn’t yet explained. In simple terms, our low-trust era should preclude incumbents from winning. But Obama won twice. George W. Bush won twice. But Hillary Clinton lost, and Harris lost. Why?

Clinton strategist, James Carville, famously said in 1992 "It's the economy, stupid," suggesting that personal finance was a fundamental driver of voter decisions. Yet, Obama won 2012 with unemployment at 7.8%, the worst of the seven cycles below. Gore lost 2000 with unemployment at 3.9%, the best. Clinton (2016, 4.6%) and Harris (2024, 4.2%) also lost on healthy economies. If the economy explained the gap in our trust thesis, Gore should have easily won and Obama should have lost.51

While the current era is defined by low trust, trust is still a moving measure, and with it goes the anti-incumbency bias we would expect. Instead of considering the low-trust era as the only indicator, we should instead consider institutional trust as a temporary frame or "regime" - an influence on elections but not a guarantee. In high-trust eras (the Reagan-Bush peak), the electorate rewarded continuity, and incumbents and chosen successors won. In low-trust eras (2008–present), the electorate rewarded change, and challengers and outsider-positioned candidates typically won. The rule isn’t "the change candidate always wins." It’s "the regime-favored frame wins," and the regime determines which frame is favored.52

The table above might seem tediously long and even a bit tautological. A cursory glance might make one assume we worked backwards to name the regime based on the results. Not quite. There is an independent variable that actually drives our regime fit. Start with the left-hand trust column. In years of measured high trust, the regime is "continuity." The winner is almost always the continuity candidate. In years of low trust, the regime is "change" and the winner is almost always the change candidate. This makes institutional trust, a compound measure pollsters can estimate well in advance of the election, a reasonably strong predictor of presidential results.

The rule, and who fires it

The pattern revealed in the Figure W table is, however, just a pattern, not a causal mechanism. The rule we’re describing here has to be triggered by some specific kind of voter, because not all voters care about an anti-system framing equally. And, more importantly, not all votes count the same because our electoral college biases national outcomes to specific state outcomes. That means to understand our causal mechanism, we really need only look to the swing-state Critic: a System Critic living in a contested state. This is a voter who distrusts government and hasn’t already locked in on partisan grounds. What do they do when handed two candidates? They pick the one they rate as more honest, relative to the in-power party they’re currently angry at. Figure X specifically draws out the swing state candidate honesty rating. In years where there is a statistical difference in honesty ratings between candidates, the honesty metric always picks the winner.

§17 · The regime under the rule

The regime sets the board

We now know that the regime rule is real, and the swing-state Critic is the one who triggers it. We also know from prior sections that a candidate's fit to a regime, their authenticity, is decided well before the general election. But even then, the candidate's fit is worth only a point or two. So how does the regime then matter to elections? What has changed to make trust so indicative of electoral results? Simple. The margin changed.

When the rule sat idle

For most of the postwar period, our trust regime rule sat idle, denoted by the "High-trust era" in Figure Y. Through the 1980s and 1990s the party in power, particularly incumbents running for second terms, usually opened the election season a few points clear of fifty. That gave them a cushion to absorb almost any candidate-vs-regime mismatch without anyone noticing; a flat continuity pick or a sharp change challenger might cost a point, never the election. And more importantly, the candidates were all "of the system" and the system was trusted, so trust was less of a discernible characteristic. In a sense, the trust pattern held the whole time, but rarely was a race close enough to make it matter. And rarely did a candidate authenticity squarely delineate them.

Figure Y also shows a the start of a transition in trust in 2008, with a large drop-off in 2016. That collapse did two things at once, and both of them shrink what a campaign can change. It pinned the in-power party’s starting margin right at the edge of fifty, and it flipped who bothers to show up at all.

The margin collapse

In every high-trust cycle the in-power party opened above the 50% popular-vote share comfortably, somewhere around 51 to 55%, which gave the continuity candidate a cushion: room to absorb a small shove in one direction and still win. In every low-trust cycle, almost every election since 2008, that baseline has collapsed to 47–51%. The in-power party’s vote share simply isn’t running high enough to bank any cushion anymore, and the electorate has settled into a very tight margin, for reasons we’ll get into. A tight margin also doesn’t keep: whatever cushion a winner takes office with tends to thin across the four years of a term, so by the next election it’s gone, and the race is run from a near-tie.

The turnout flip

It isn’t simply that the trust regime changes the starting margin of a campaign; it also shapes how voters show up. Note the baseline shift of our mean trust composite, down into the below-0.3 range shown in Figure Z. By separating elections as below or above 0.38, a U-distribution starts to appear. On the right side, in the high-trust regime, we get the classic civic-engagement model. That model, which political science has taught for fifty years, says trust drives turnout: engaged voters who believe institutions work are the ones who vote. The classic model held pretty well for a long time. But every election from 2008 on reveals an inverted relationship, one where rising trust seems to decrease turnout.

The hierarchy of effects

Put these pieces together and the trust regime sorts a campaign's forces into a simple but unintuitive hierarchy. The biggest force is the one a candidate cannot touch, and the force that decides elections is a small one they can.

First, the regime: the structural setup a candidate inherits before the campaign begins, largely outside of the control of the candidate. This squarely aligns with existing political science work. Alan Abramowitz’s Time for Change model predicts the in-power party’s two-party vote share months ahead, from the president’s June approval, second-quarter growth, and a penalty for a party reaching for a third straight term.53 Run that model across the postwar era and it almost does all the work. It called a clear Obama win in 2008 and a one-point Obama win in 2012, both right, and read Trump’s 2020 defeat off his minus-fifteen approval. The model explains roughly 86% of the variance in the postwar popular vote, which is a lot. But the 2020 call only held because it threw out the pandemic-blown GDP input and leaned on approval alone, and in 2016 and 2024 it missed outright. By an honest read, then, the Abramowitz model hasn’t cleanly called an election since 2012. Our thesis is that the model is indirectly predicated on the classic turnout model. And its failure in the last several elections is due to the low trust regime change. With distrust close to uniform now, the in-power party’s old buffer is gone and every recent race runs close, and a close race is no longer settled on the national number the model predicts. Something has shifted in the post-Obama era that makes the 14% variance the forecast cannot explain matter far more than it used to. The regime still sets the board; it just longer picks who wins.

Second, the candidate’s fit to that regime: small on the national scoreboard, and decisive anyway. The gap the Abramowitz forecast leaves is only a point or two, but in a knife-edge era a point or two is the whole election. Where fit then shows up most critically is in electoral geography. In 2016 the fundamentals called the in-power Democrat vulnerable, Clinton beat the national forecast and won the popular vote by two points, and she lost anyway, because the establishment-misfit penalty wasn’t spread evenly. It pooled in the handful of states that decide the Electoral College, among the swing-state Critics who flipped them. A poorly fit candidate doesn’t lose the country by a little everywhere; they lose it exactly where the margin is thin enough to matter. Fit is also why some change wins are routs and others squeakers: a novel outsider like Obama in 2008 or Trump in 2016 hands voters a blank slate to project a clean break onto, and the regime’s gift arrives in full, while a restoration figure like Biden in 2020 or the former-president Trump of 2024 offers change as reversion to a known administration, which can only cash so much of it. Fit is also the lever a party sets first and sets hardest, in the nomination. If a party nominates the wrong candidate, their poor fit is a headwind that blows the length of the race, and very little run from inside a campaign can turn back into it. Fit ultimately beats campaign hustle.

Third, the campaign itself, which splits in two. The persuasion half, the messaging and the ad buys and the pivot to the center, is the measured zero from earlier: it changes few minds and sums to less than the forecast’s own margin of error. The mobilization half earns its keep: a disciplined ground game, the door-knocks and the field program, is the one campaign act with a real effect on the result. Contact beats content. But contact and ground game still only work at the margin, and they meet the same wall as everything else. A great ground game leans into a good fit and runs up the score; it does not drag a badly-fit candidate into a better outcome. In fact, increased contact for a bad-fit candidate might drive greater defection by inadvertently contacting System Critic voters with System Believer candidate outreach. Basically, the best field operation in the country cannot save a candidate that the regime is set against.

So the hierarchy is simple and blunt. The regime sets the board, and you inherit it. A pivot to the center changes nothing. A ground game moves the margin but cannot reverse a bad fit. Fit decides. A party that wants to win picks for fit, not for purity, and it picks in the nomination, before the campaign likely has a national footing. That is why the next sections put fit on a visual map, placing voters and candidates in the same 2D space of trust and ideology so you can see for yourself how closely historic candidates overlap the bloc that actually decides a cycle. These 2D maps document who specifically decided 2016, 2020, and 2024.

§19 · What this means for “electability”

A match, not a trait

Step back and look at the three elections we just stepped through. In all three, the candidate the Democratic Party (and much of the press) had settled on as the safe, “electable” choice54 was the one who lost the swing-state System Critics and, with them, the election. Biden’s 2020 victory was so narrow against a historically low-approval incumbent that it undercuts any claim of an overwhelming electoral mandate and rejects the notion that his electability was self-evident. In two of the three cycles, the Democrat repeatedly labeled “most electable” proved least able to reach the voters who ultimately decided the election. These were not political novices, nor were they underfunded. If the same pattern keeps recurring, perhaps the flaw lies less with the candidates than with how “electability” itself is defined.

The pundit-class question, “is this candidate electable?”, is effectively malformed. A candidate is not electable in the abstract. They are electable conditional on a regime. Hillary Clinton was a plausible nominee against, say, Jeb Bush in a high-trust election. She was structurally unelectable as the maximum-insider candidate during a low-trust anti-establishment regime. Same candidate, same résumé, two different regimes, two different verdicts. Kamala Harris reached the 2024 nomination for one reason: she was the sitting vice president. In a high-trust continuity cycle that credential is an asset; in a low-trust change cycle it is not. In 2024 she carried 100% of Joe Biden’s regime-mismatch baggage and the maximum-insider position the electorate was already primed to reject.

The implication runs uphill into nomination strategy. The party that wins close cycles in the low-trust era is the party whose nominee positions themselves outside the system that the electorate is angry at. That is not a left-right axis. It is an inside-outside axis. Mitt Romney, Bernie Sanders, Donald Trump, and Pete Buttigieg occupy radically different positions on policy, but Trump and Sanders both sit outside the system, while Romney and Buttigieg both sit inside it. The electorate sorts on the latter axis first, and only then, if it has any energy left, on the former.

The Democratic Party’s repeated misfit is not ideological. It is a selection mechanism. The party’s nomination process (endorsements, donor networks, party-elder coordination) is engineered to surface candidates with the strongest insider markers: Senate seats, cabinet appointments, donor-network density, party-elder blessing. And those are precisely the markers that bar a candidate from credibly carrying the change frame in a low-trust regime. The Republican Party’s chaotic open primaries have, by accident, twice produced a regime-matching candidate in the low-trust era because the same chaos that horrifies their own elites also lets a genuine outsider through. Democrats select for insider competence; Republicans select for whoever wins the brawl. When the regime rewards change, insider competence is structurally disqualifying.

§20 · The bad trade

Losing your base

We’ve shown who won over the cohorts of disaffected voters that effectively decided the last several election cycles. And through the cross-cycle trust pattern and our frame-match scoreboard, we also showed why establishment candidates, particularly Democrats, keep losing. So why then do seasoned campaign managers and experienced politicians keep chasing the middle? Because the data on swing voters, when read naively, sets a logical trap.

Imagine you’re planning a 2028 presidential campaign. You’re in the war room and you pull up the ANES data on swing voters. Voters who put themselves at positions 3–5 on ANES’s 7-point liberal-conservative scale represent about 20% of the electorate. The bad news is that only 26% of those self-declared moderates actually swung their vote from what their self-described partisanship would have predicted. But the good news is that it’s the highest swing rate of any ideological slice. It works out to 5% of the entire electorate (20% × 26%), the biggest bucket of swing voters on the board.55

So the obvious play is just as you suspected: tack to the center and scoop them up. And conventional wisdom backs you up. It’s what pundits say, and it’s also what campaigns have been doing for a long time. In July 2016, Chuck Schumer even said it out loud: “For every blue-collar Democrat we lose in western Pennsylvania, we will pick up two moderate Republicans in the suburbs in Philadelphia, and you can repeat that in Ohio and Illinois and Wisconsin.”58 Clinton then lost Pennsylvania, Ohio, and Wisconsin, carrying only Illinois. Schumer’s bet might have paid off before 2016, but the data now says that moving to the center actively costs you.

First, the “middle” (as a loose, incoherent, self-identified group) is the hardest mass to actually move: there’s no distinct handle to grab it by. Those self-described moderate swing voters score only 0.22 on the standard 0–1 trust composite (a combined score of three ANES items measuring trust in government, where 1 = full trust and 0 = none). But that’s basically the national average; the national mean was about 0.23 in 2016.55 So they aren’t a distinct trust bloc, high or low; they sit right at the national level, like everyone else. And their ideology isn’t cohesive either, so there’s no single policy that moves them, as we explored earlier. Average on trust, incoherent on ideology: there’s no lever that singles them out.

They’re the largest swing bucket because they’re the most poorly-defined, by outside perception and probably by their own internal positions. They’re voters whose vote isn’t strongly explained by their ideology, their trust, or their policies. They’re deciding for other reasons: maybe fundamentals, maybe candidate likeability, maybe they’re just noise in the polling system, or maybe it’s all just the vagaries of human preference on any given election day.

Second, and this is the real killer of the centrist trap: your own camp’s swing voters are the distrustful ones. Picture the voters a Democrat actually loses at the margin. They called themselves lean-left in 2016, and then they did not pull the lever for Clinton. On the same 0–1 trust scale, this group scores 0.17, the lowest reading of anyone inside the Democratic tent, below the moderate swing voters at 0.22 and well below the loyal strong-left at 0.27.56 These are not mystery voters. They are the same anti-establishment cohort that the Sanders→Trump arithmetic turned up, the defectors plus the roughly 30% of Sanders primary voters who simply stayed home. They are voters who are anti-establishment at the core and only incidentally left-leaning, and who were never going to vote for Clinton in the first place. The VSG populism data backs the distinction: this cohort runs substantially more populist than the Obama→Clinton loyalists who stuck around, and what defines them is the populism, not the progressivism. Which is why every step a candidate takes toward the center, every move that makes them look more moderate, more establishment, more “safe,” is a step structurally further from where this anti-establishment cohort’s trust actually sits, not a step closer. We cannot say with panel evidence that centrist positioning causes lean-left defection, but the structural-distance argument is empirical.57

§21 · Why the party keeps making the bet

Crowning the insider

Why then does the Democratic party keep reaching for the safe candidate when the safe candidate is the one who loses? Because it is built to. In Clayton Christensen’s Innovator’s Dilemma, a market leader fails not because its managers are foolish but because they are disciplined: listening to their best existing customers and defending their most profitable lines is exactly what makes them cede the disruptive new lane until it is too late.59 A party built to protect its own incumbency behaves the same way, and the logic cuts in both directions: when it is winning it sees no reason to change, and when it is losing it decides the moment is too risky to try anything new. Its endorsement networks, donor lists, and superdelegate machinery are all tuned to reward the candidate the institution already trusts, which in a low-trust era is the candidate the electorate has already written off.

You can watch it correct one cycle and tilt the next. In 2008 the establishment had its heir: Hillary Clinton entered as the inevitable frontrunner, holding a nearly three-to-one lead among superdelegates before a single vote was cast.60 But superdelegates were only a third of the math, and as Obama, the insurgent, built a lead in pledged delegates, the party’s insiders fell in behind the voters; the open process corrected itself, and Obama won the nomination and the presidency. By 2016 the tilt had hardened. The DNC’s own leaked emails showed officials working to undercut Sanders, and when his supporters sued, the party’s lawyer told a federal court that the charter’s neutrality pledge was unenforceable, that the DNC could have picked its nominee in a back room over cigars if it wanted. The judge dismissed the suit for lack of standing, never reaching whether that pledge bound the party.61 Clinton took the nomination and lost the general.

By 2024 the party was rewriting the rules in advance: at Joe Biden’s request, the DNC reordered its calendar to make South Carolina the first contest, the state that resurrected his 2020 campaign. The stated reason was diversity, but South Carolina is neither the most diverse state nor a competitive one, and it has not voted Democratic in a presidential general since 1976; a diverse battleground would have served that goal better. What the South Carolina move rewarded is standing with the party’s most loyal, establishment base. Then, when Biden withdrew, the party skipped a real contest and installed the vetted successor anyway62 — a procedural choice that minimized anti-establishment dissent.

What would have happened in 2024 with a rapid jungle primary? Or a contested convention? Maybe Harris would have emerged the victor, but the fact remains that the Democratic Party took serious steps to avoid any chance that she wouldn't. That is the establishment protecting itself — a political Innovator's Dilemma.

And the machinery does not even need a written rule. Look at the logic of who quit the primary race, and when, in 2020. Through the first three contests Biden had won nothing, finishing fourth in Iowa, fifth in New Hampshire, and a distant second in Nevada, while Buttigieg led the moderate lane after effectively winning Iowa. Then James Clyburn endorsed Biden, South Carolina’s loyalists followed, and the dynamic changed overnight. CNN headlined its exit-poll story “Black voters, older voters and moderates fuel Joe Biden’s victory in South Carolina.” Biden’s first win of the entire race ran on the oldest, most moderate, most religious Black electorate of the early states, a South Carolina electorate that looks nothing like Black Democrats nationally, among whom, at that very moment, Sanders actually led Biden 35% to 30%. And yet, within forty-eight hours, on the night before Super Tuesday, Buttigieg and Klobuchar both dropped out and endorsed Biden, the man who had just won his first contest ever, in an open effort to close ranks and stop Sanders.

The only other candidate who could have done the same for the left did the opposite: Warren, who had faded just as badly as Buttigieg or Klobuchar, stayed in and split her voters with Sanders through Super Tuesday. The early moderate leader stepped aside for a candidate who had won nothing until that week, and the one person who could have cleared the progressive lane refused to. That is not voter preference sorting itself out. Reporting pointed to establishment pressure and an inferred “hidden hand” from Barack Obama, even as Biden denied any organized effort, and because this version ran through phone calls and endorsements instead of emails and court filings, it left no paper trail. The establishment protects its own.63

If the machinery that keeps reaching for the center cannot reliably pick a winner, then what does winning actually look like? How can a candidate own the outsider lane?

§22 · Two ways to win the outsider lane

Discipline vs Flood

There are two empirically-tested ways to claim the outsider lane, and they look completely different. Bernie Sanders ran on relentless discipline: one policy, never bent. Donald Trump ran on the absence of any discipline at all: contradictory statements daily, no message control, total chaos. Each works. Both produce the same outcome, voters rating the candidate as more authentic, more trustworthy, more “for me” than the establishment alternative.

Bernie’s discipline: one policy, never bent. Bernie made Medicare for All his central frame across 2016 and 2020, and he refused to dilute the single-payer story when pressed. As the 2020 field filled with softer alternatives, Buttigieg’s “Medicare for All Who Want It,” Harris’s shifting public-option hybrid, Biden’s flat opposition, Sanders never retreated to a public option or hedged on ending private insurance.64 The discipline reads as: “I won’t sell out on this one thing. The position you see today is the position you’ll see tomorrow.”

The Overton-window evidence is unambiguous. Medicare for All wasn’t a polled mainstream Democratic position before Bernie’s 2016 campaign. The Kaiser Family Foundation began tracking it in 2017 specifically because Sanders made it a national issue.65 By 2020 every major Democratic primary candidate had to take a position: Sanders (full single-payer), Warren (full single-payer with evolving cost details), Harris (shifted between full and public-option), Buttigieg (“Medicare for All Who Want It” = public option), Biden (public option only, opposed M4A). KFF polling in 2020 showed roughly 56% of Americans favoring a national health plan and 61% support among independents. One disciplined outsider, one policy, two cycles. The policy menu for an entire party shifted.

The other half of Bernie’s discipline: refusing the bait. Holding one policy is the visible part. The invisible part is refusing to engage the culture-war framings designed to drag you onto unfavorable terrain. Sanders’ reflex, almost regardless of the question, was to redirect to economic class: the real issue is the greed of the billionaire class, a rigged economy, whether you can afford to see a doctor. Asked even to define his own “democratic socialism,” he answered with FDR, Social Security, and Medicare rather than litigate the label on his opponents’ terms.64 He didn’t grant week-long interviews relitigating each wedge issue; he kept the economy the conversation and let the rest slide past.

This second discipline matters because the trap of culture-war attacks isn’t whether you win the argument. It’s whether that argument becomes the conversation. McCombs and Shaw’s foundational 1972 paper on agenda-setting established that media coverage shapes which issues voters think about, independently of changing voter opinions on those issues.66 So when a candidate engages with a culture-war attack, even to refute it, the news cycle covers the issue for additional days. The candidate has helped the opponent set the agenda. The disciplined response isn’t “here’s my correct position on this wedge issue.” It’s “I’m not going to answer that. Let’s talk about Medicare for All.” You cannot have a culture-war conversation if one side refuses to participate.

Establishment Democrats consistently fail this discipline. Clinton 2016 on emails and Benghazi engaged at length (11 hours of House Benghazi testimony in 2015, extensive interviews, ongoing campaign statements). Harris 2024 on transgender care for ICE detainees responded with carefully crafted positions instead of refusing the frame, even after Trump’s “Kamala is for they/them” ad had been running for weeks. Biden 2024 proposed policy after policy on immigration in response to GOP attacks instead of redirecting to economic populism. The establishment instinct is to respond, explain, defend. That instinct hands the opponent the agenda.

Trump’s flood: no discipline at all. Trump took the opposite approach. He said contradictory things daily. He reversed positions mid-rally. He attacked his own administration. His former chief strategist Steve Bannon described the mechanism in a 2018 interview with Michael Lewis: “The Democrats don’t matter. The real opposition is the media. And the way to deal with them is to flood the zone with shit.”67 The mechanism isn’t persuasion. It’s disorientation. Voters can’t track every contradiction, so they fall back on perception of authenticity.

The numbers show how it works in practice. Trump’s honesty rating in 2016 averaged 33–36% (Gallup), among the lowest ever recorded for a presidential candidate. For comparison: Bush 65%, Obama 61%, Clinton (Bill) 46%. But Hillary Clinton in 2016 was at 32%; Trump and Clinton were tied at the floor.68 On absolute honesty, neither candidate was credible. On relative honesty against the establishment Democrat, the honesty crossover shows System Critics rating Trump more honest than Clinton in 2016 and Harris in 2024. The flood works precisely because the establishment can’t credibly claim honesty either. When everyone is suspected of lying, the candidate who openly defies norms reads as more authentic than the candidate who carefully crafts every statement.

Why both work. Both strategies are anti-establishment signals; they just signal different specific things. Bernie’s discipline signals: “I have a fixed identity. I won’t be moved by donors or party leadership.” Trump’s chaos signals: “I won’t be controlled by the system at all. I can’t be bought because I can’t be predicted.” Both signal “the system doesn’t own me.” In a low-trust era, that signal is the change-lane currency.

Strategic implication. A candidate trying to hold the outsider lane has to pick a mechanism. Establishment Democrats typically try neither. They want to be pragmatic on policy (changing positions to fit polls and donor preferences) AND respectable and in-control (avoiding chaos, sticking to talking points). That combination signals of the system in two ways at once: pragmatism without discipline reads as opportunism, and discipline without willingness to break things reads as bureaucracy. Holding the outsider lane requires either Bernie’s discipline (pick one policy, hold it through every attack) or Trump’s chaos (accept that you can’t be the calm, vetted, talking-point candidate).

Caveats. Discipline is rarer and harder than chaos. Bernie’s MFA discipline required ignoring decades of donor-class advice to “moderate.” Chaos has costs this essay doesn’t quantify: low-information environments, harder governance, increased polarization. The descriptive claim here is about election mechanics, not a normative claim about which strategy is better. Both mechanisms produced specific empirical wins (Bernie shifted the Democratic policy menu; Trump won 2016 and 2024) AND specific empirical losses (Bernie lost both primaries; Trump lost 2020 as the incumbent). The mechanisms work for anti-establishment positioning. They don’t work for incumbents who have become the system (Trump 2020).

§23 · What the press should measure

Donors, not dollars

A candidate who can hold the outsider lane can win the general. A party’s own machinery is often built to keep them off the ballot. That leaves one institution positioned to surface them anyway: the political press. Not because the press picks nominees (the party’s consolidation machinery does that) but because the press writes the “electability” story that consolidation runs on. Decide who is “serious” and “viable” and you set the terms every donor, endorser, and wavering primary voter prices in. And right now the press builds that verdict from the wrong numbers, filing a candidate’s record donor base under human-interest color while the endorsement and dollar tallies carry the “electable” stamp.

The press evaluates primary candidates using a familiar set of diagnostics: endorsements, fundraising totals, polling among likely primary voters, name recognition, and, in the Democratic Party, superdelegate support. Those metrics are designed to predict who will win the primary under the party’s current selection rules. They are not designed to predict who will win the general election in a low-trust regime. In fact, they can point in exactly the wrong direction for that outcome. The very insider signals that help a candidate dominate a primary are often the same signals that swing-state Critics interpret as evidence that the candidate is part of the system they want to reject. If the press instead evaluated candidates using the framework proposed here, it would likely have written about a different "frontrunner" in nearly every recent cycle.

Superdelegates were a death sentence. Even after the 2018 reforms reducing their first-ballot voting power, the existence of a party-elder override mechanism communicated to voters that the establishment had a thumb on the scale. In 2016 specifically, the superdelegate count was used by the press as an "inevitability" narrative starting in mid-2015, eighteen months before any primary vote was cast. That narrative is precisely the kind of insider-signal that disqualifies a candidate in a change-favored regime. Calling out and removing such mechanisms isn’t process reform; it’s regime fit.

Total campaign contributions in dollars is the wrong metric. A candidate raising $50M from 200 corporate donors at $250,000 each has activated 200 lobbyists. A candidate raising $50M from 2 million donors at $25 each has activated 2 million voters. Both report "raised $50M." The press treats those as equivalent. They are not. The first signals access to wealth; the second signals coalition engagement. And access to wealth is not benign; the concentrated elite influence those big checks represent is exactly what bends policy ( as we will discuss next in §25). Engagement is what predicts turnout, which is the dominant variable in a low-trust regime where the electorate is mobilized by anti-system anger rather than by faith in the system (Figure T).

We propose four better metrics, all of which directly map onto regime-fit predictions and all of which the political press could report tomorrow:

  • Number of unique donors. Predicts turnout breadth. A candidate with broader donor base is, mechanically, a candidate who has activated more voters at the cost of admission ($1+). Donor count is a direct proxy for the size of the engaged base: the population that will actually turn out and bring others.
  • Number of first-time donors (or donors who have not given to a state or federal candidate in the prior 4+ years). Predicts coalition expansion. A candidate whose donor base is a re-activation of the same coalition that lost the prior cycle is, by definition, not expanding the electorate. A candidate who is pulling in new donors is bringing in voters the prior coalition didn’t reach. That cohort overlaps directly with the swing-state Critics §16 identified.
  • Cross-coalition donor count. Number of donors who have previously given to other parties' candidates, registered independent, or appear in voter files with mixed-ballot history. Predicts the candidate’s cross-coalition appeal in a way endorsement counts cannot: endorsements come from inside the party, donors can come from anywhere.
  • Swing-state geographic distribution of donors. Percentage of unique donors residing in PA, MI, WI, AZ, GA, NC, NV. A candidate with 80% of donors in CA, NY, MA, and DC is raising money where it cannot move the EC. A candidate with broader swing geography is raising it from the voters who actually decide the cycle (the swing-state Critics §16 identified).

These four metrics aren’t novel. Campaigns already track them because they predict electoral performance. What is novel is treating them as the primary metrics of a campaign, rather than burying them beneath endorsement counts and fundraising headlines. By donor count and small-dollar share, the two metrics we can independently verify from public records, Sanders led the Democratic field decisively in both 2016 and 2020. The campaigns’ internal coalition data suggests the same pattern across the remaining metrics. By the press’s preferred measures, however, Clinton in 2016 was the inevitable nominee, and Biden in 2020 was the establishment candidate behind whom the party ultimately consolidated. The selection mechanism delivered both nominations to the candidate the regime was least likely to reward. We cannot prove Sanders would have won either general election. We can show that the metrics which would have identified him as the stronger regime fit were precisely the ones the press relegated beneath endorsements, fundraising totals, and other establishment signals.

The prescription for political journalism is straightforward. Stop leading with endorsement counts, fundraising totals, and party-elite support. Lead instead with unique donor count, first-time donor percentage, cross-coalition donor count, and swing-state donor geography. A press corps that reports these four metrics will consistently identify the candidates best aligned with the political regime, regardless of which faction of the party they come from. A press corps that doesn't will continue elevating regime-misfit nominees, then act surprised when the trust-regime model predicts the outcome more accurately than its own conventional indicators.

§24 · The cultural-progressive trap

The label they put on you

Owning the outsider lane is the one move this whole essay says actually works. But there are two traps that we have not discussed that still stand in the way, and neither is about policy; both are about language. The first trap is the label other people put on you. A candidate who claims the lane cleanly still wears the brand of their party, whatever their own positions. For Democrats, our running example, that brand reads as culturally progressive, “woke,” and pulls them onto the one axis where the outsider advantage dies: cultural issues.

The “woke” brand is a heavy liability with exactly the working-class voters Democrats need, and it attaches to the party regardless of any one candidate’s positions. On the label itself, the polling isn’t close:

  1. 84% of Americans say it’s a serious problem that people can’t speak freely in everyday situations for fear of retaliation (NYT/Siena, March 2022).69
  2. 57% said “political correctness or being ‘woke’” was important to the 2024 election outcome (YouGov, November 2024).70
  3. Yet the underlying salience is symmetric: 53% call left-wing extremism a “major problem” and 52% say the same about right-wing extremism (Pew, September 2025).71
  4. 69% of voters say Democrats are “too focused on being politically correct” (Navigator Research internal Democratic polling, March 2025; bias-disclosed in the note).72
  5. Working-class voters describe the Democratic brand as “woke, weak, and out-of-touch” (Democracy Matters / American Bridge 21-state project, 2025; bias-disclosed in the note).73

But the label only sticks to one side. The Democratic Party absorbs the “left-wing” tag whether or not its candidates support those positions, while the Republican Party has shed the “right-wing” tag from its mass brand even while running Trump and his post-2020 movement.

Both parties exploit this. Republicans run against “woke Dems” every cycle. Trump’s 2024 “Kamala is for they/them, President Trump is for you” ad is the textbook play, reportedly Trump’s largest single TV spend of the cycle.74 And establishment Democrats use the same attacks against progressive primary challengers. Clinton 2016 against Bernie attacked his 2005 PLCAA gun vote (framed as “siding with the NRA” against Sandy Hook families) precisely to sort Bernie onto the culture-war axis where the progressive economic-populist coalition would fragment.75

That’s the cultural trap. The party brand’s progressive-cultural baggage tags any Democratic candidate as culture-war-targetable, regardless of the candidate’s own positions. Conservative messengers can use it. Establishment Democrats can use it against progressive primary challengers. Refusing to engage (the attentional discipline from the outsider-lane playbook) is the only credible defense, and even then it is a defense, not an escape: the brand puts every Democrat on trial for positions that aren’t their own.

§25 · The vocabulary trap (and the fiscal-conservatism myth)

The label you put on yourself

The second trap is the label you put on yourself. The groups we sort ourselves into, the words we choose to say about who we are, carry real weight: they shape how we vote and whom we trust. But the grouping can deceive, because the word and the reality underneath it drift apart. There’s an old idea in philosophy, usually credited to the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein, that a word has no fixed, true meaning of its own; it means whatever people use it to mean.76 And the use drifts, often by design, because whoever drives the conversation drives the usage, and so, in time, the meaning. A label holds its shape only for as long as the people steering the debate keep it there. Take today’s labels at face value and you will misread the very voters you are chasing; and a candidate they trust, however, can pull a label somewhere new, the way we already saw Trump move his own voters’ positions for them.

Self-grouping cuts the other way, too, and we have already seen its force: the surest way to raise turnout is to get someone to think of themselves as a voter, because claiming the label is part of becoming one. The names we give ourselves do real work.

Take abortion. To much of the right, “pro-choice” has come to mean someone who would allow abortion late in pregnancy, even, in the most lurid telling, “after birth.” Both are caricatures. Abortions after 21 weeks are about one percent of the total, and a “post-birth abortion” is not a procedure that exists; it would be infanticide, and it is illegal everywhere. “Pro-life,” for its part, stretches from barring abortion after a reasonable point all the way to banning contraception. Underneath the two labels sits the thing they hide: a broad, stable consensus. Only about a quarter of Americans say abortion should be legal in all cases, only about one in twelve say it should be illegal in all cases, and a clear majority land in between, legal with limits, more permissive early in pregnancy and more restrictive late.77 The issue is genuinely nuanced. The labels swallow the nuance whole.

But in that context, watch how this played out with “oligarchy.” After 2024, some Democrats decided the word itself was the problem. Senator Elissa Slotkin told Politico it doesn’t land “outside of coastal institutions,” and urged the party to say “kings” instead; Mitch Landrieu’s Working Class Project made the same case, with Landrieu reporting that “not one person in all of our focus groups mentioned the word ‘oligarchy.’” But the evidence that voters can’t follow the word is thin. Asked to pick its definition out of a lineup, large majorities got it right, including 68% of independents; told what it means, 60% agreed the country is at least somewhat oligarchic. And Sanders ran an entire “Fighting Oligarchy” tour to some of the biggest crowds in the country.78 The concern was never missing. The fight was over the word, and the word is exactly the kind of thing a candidate with enough standing can move: Sanders was not introducing himself to those crowds, he was introducing the term, and the argument behind it. That a word has not surfaced in a focus group yet is no proof the belief behind it isn’t there; reading a missing word as a missing concern gets the whole thing backwards. Conservative messengers route around the same gap with their own anti-elite framings (“drain the swamp,” “they took our jobs”) that tap the same sentiment in different words. The change-lane message has to be economic, and in language voters actually use.

Working-class voters DO believe the system is captured. Independent polling is unambiguous. Pew Research (July 2023, n=8,480): 80% say donors have too much influence on Congress; 73% say the same about lobbyists / special interests; 70% say constituents have too little. Pew (February 2024): 62% say reducing money’s influence should be a top priority, with bipartisan support (65% Democrats, 60% Republicans). Pew 2024 wealth-gap polling: 51% call the wealth gap a “very big problem” and another 32% call it “moderately big.” Politico (2025): 61% say billionaires have too much political influence. And it isn’t only sentiment: the most-cited empirical study of American policymaking, across 1,779 policy questions, found that the preferences of average citizens have close to zero independent effect on what becomes law, while the preferences of economic elites and organized business interests largely drive it.79 The underlying anti-concentration-of-wealth sentiment is real, bipartisan, and well-documented across non-partisan sources. A party that refuses to label itself with the language of economic equity is missing a huge opportunity.

The fiscal-restraint half doesn’t map to what either party actually does. Perception is not: the “fiscally conservative” brand stuck to the Republican Party through every one of those deficit-expanding administrations.

This pattern shouldn’t be politically possible if voters were responding to actual fiscal behavior. But voter perception still maps “fiscally conservative” to the Republican Party. The label has become a cultural marker (responsibility, sobriety, anti-welfare, anti-spending-on-the-poor) rather than a description of deficit/debt behavior. The “socially liberal, fiscally conservative” voter is typically a Democratic-leaning upper-middle-class professional whose actual policy preferences include high government spending on healthcare, education, infrastructure, and child care, funded by taxes on higher incomes. By any traditional definition, that is fiscally liberal, the exact opposite of the label they claim. The self-description doesn’t match the policy preferences; it signals cultural alignment with what a fiscal-restraint voter is supposed to look like.

The Democratic Socialist Boogeyman. Labels can also change hands. A handful of recent primary victories by candidates backed by New York’s new Democratic Socialist mayor, Zohran Mamdani, sent Democratic leaders into a visible and very public panic81 Within days, thirteen House members signed a centrist pledge, “Promise to America,” declaring themselves “capitalist, not socialist.“82 But compare that pledge to the substance of what they are rejecting. The most popular programs in the country are, notably, the “socialist” ones: Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid, each supported by three-quarters or more of the public.83 So are many of the most popular proposals for new policy: allowing Medicare to negotiate drug prices, raising the minimum wage, and increasing taxes on income over $400,000.84 In their rush to disavow democratic socialism, centrist Democrats cede the frame. They let the right define “socialism” as whatever the slur needs to mean, and in the process surrender ownership of a long list of policies the public already supports at the ballot box. If the word socialist is as politically powerful as Democratic elites seem to believe, it is striking how readily they abandon it. How do they plan to reconcile their pious devotion to “capitalism” with the enduring popularity of these supposedly socialist programs? And if, as we’ve shown throughout this essay, there is no coherent political “center” waiting to be won over, then whose support are they really trying to attract? More importantly, whose donations are they trying to keep?

And all of this language navigation is hardest for a big-tent party. The label “Democrat” has to stretch over a South Carolina Democrat and a Southern California one, a Minnesotan, a Louisianan, a New Yorker. The words each would use for the very same position are shaped by the place they live. There is no single Democratic dialect.

The Democratic tent is a coalition of competing narratives. A candidate who wants to occupy the change lane, then, cannot speak in the political class’s assumed vocabulary. They cannot rely on insider words built for an insider’s map of the country. The job is to find the common-denominator language, the words that carry across all of those places, and speak in those. And if there is one narrative that comes closest to spanning the entire coalition, it is distrust in the system. Arguably, it is the closest thing the Democratic Party has to a common language, if it were ever willing to embrace it.

Part VII

The bottom line

What all of this adds up to, and the trap that remains.

Coda

The center was never a place to run to.

All of our analysis comes back to the number we opened with: in 1964, three in four Americans trusted their government; today, one in five do. Almost everything in this essay falls out of that one collapse. The center stopped being a place a candidate could run toward, because the voters who actually decide elections were never sitting in a tidy moderate middle. They are switchers and no-shows, scattered across the spectrum, and what moves them is not policy. The policy story has been tested directly, across dozens of field experiments, and it comes back a measured zero: between two known candidates, all the ads and door-knocks in the world move vote choice by nothing.

What moves voters comes in two flavors: changing their choice of candidate, and changing their willingness to turn out on election day. But choice does not run on policy, or even on candidate likeability. It runs on trust and identity, on whether a candidate reads as one of your own, which is why the same act looks like “telling it like it is” from one candidate and “she’ll say anything” from another. Turnout runs on stakes and contact, on giving a distrustful electorate a reason to show up, not on making it love your nominee. Underneath both sits the regime: a low-trust era hands the change lane to whoever is visibly not the system and punishes whoever gets cast as it. That is why the establishment’s safe, electable pick keeps losing the distrustful majority; and why the Democratic Party’s machinery, built to reward the insider, keeps picking the very candidate the era turns away. So “move to the center” is not the safe half-step it looks like. It misses the persuadable voter, who was never in the middle; and it deflates the distrustful base voter, who needed a reason to show up. One move, two losses.

The outsider lane is real, but a steady supply of credible outsiders has never existed, and structurally so. It is very hard to spend a career fighting the establishment from inside the political machine and not eventually be read as part of it. The recent surge of anti-establishment victories on the left — Platner, Mamdani, and others — has come in Senate and local races, not presidential ones. And the presidential pipeline is precisely where the supply of outsider candidates is thinnest. The bench of senators and governors who could run as a genuine outsider shrinks every year a promising insurgent spends inside the system. The people who can most easily launch a national campaign from outside it are the ones who already own a private base of wealth and attention, the Mark Cubans and the Donald Trumps, which is its own kind of oligarchy problem. A leader who could actually be trusted, arriving from a modest business, a mayoralty, or a statehouse, almost never reaches the top of a presidential field. That is the deeper trap, and the one a figure like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez may already be falling into: the very work of fighting for the disaffected from inside the institution is what lets even her own allies relabel her as the institution. By the middle of 2026 the progressive commentator Krystal Ball, herself a voice of the populist left, was faulting Ocasio-Cortez's refusal to endorse against sitting incumbents and calling the once-insurgent a “de facto incumbent protector.”

All of this runs deeper than any single race. This essay simply asked who wins close elections, and why the institutionally safe candidate has repeatedly failed to deliver. But we hope, in our next essay, to ask the harder questions. Who is even allowed to run? Or, put more bluntly, who will the media and the donors permit to run a credible campaign at all? That answer lies upstream, in the money and media at the very top, in the concentrated wealth and fragmented press that decide which names ever reach a ballot, and in a deeper read of the Gilens-Page evidence than a footnote can hold. And just like this essay, we will bring the receipts and the data.

At his first inauguration, in 1789, George Washington called the newly formed government an “experiment entrusted to the hands of the American people.” An experiment, not a monument. Decades later, Jefferson would put it plainly: “laws and institutions must go hand in hand with the progress of the human mind.” The founders understood this. Whatever the textualists and originalists on today’s Supreme Court prefer to believe, the Constitution was built to be rewritten, built to be a living document, responsive to the people. And we have rewritten it twenty-seven times. The danger was never that the system would change. The danger is that it stops changing fast enough to keep faith with the people living inside it.

That is what a collapse in trust really measures. When the gap between concentrated power and wealth and the ordinary citizen grows wide enough, the democratic experiment stops feeling like it belongs to the people who live under it. And a democracy that no longer answers to its people is, sooner or later, a democracy that falls. A country no one believes in does not get reformed. It gets abandoned, divested from, or torn down. That is how the grand American experiment ends, not in a strict verdict, not in a poignant surrender, but in the slow, apathetic withdrawal of everyone it was entrusted to.

It dies with distrust.
